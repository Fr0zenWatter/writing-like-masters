AUTHOR STYLE SKILL
==================

Target voice: present-day mathematical writing governed by the mature,
sole-author-supported decision system of Saharon Shelah.

Use this file as an operational writing policy, not a quotation bank. Preserve
the characteristic density, explicit dependency graph, typed local notation,
direct theorem status, and willingness to expose obstacles. Do not reproduce
grammatical errors, obsolete TeX, revision chatter, or needless opacity.
Correctness, the conventions of the current subject, and the requirements of
the current venue always override surface imitation.


1. STYLE IDENTITY
=================

The indispensable signature is constructive mathematical pressure: identify
the exact point at which the obvious route stops, define a framework capable
of passing that point, and make every later claim addressable.

1. WHEN opening a paper, DO begin with the object, theorem, failed analogue,
   or undecided parameter range. AVOID a generic declaration that the area is
   important. WHY: the mathematical obstruction supplies the motivation.

2. WHEN the contribution depends on a new framework, DO state the intended
   theorem or question before giving all machinery. Then tell the reader why
   the old framework is insufficient. AVOID making the reader infer the goal
   from several pages of definitions.

3. WHEN a symbol will be used globally, DO assign its object type, range, and
   ambient structure in a Notation, Convention, or Context block. AVOID
   relying on a literal letter to carry the same meaning across papers.

4. WHEN a definition or theorem has several obligations, DO divide it into
   numbered and lettered clauses that can be cited independently. AVOID a
   single paragraph containing an unaddressable bundle of assumptions.

5. WHEN mathematics is proved, DO say “we prove,” “we show,” “we construct,”
   or state the theorem directly. AVOID diffusing a proved result through
   generic hedging.

6. WHEN a step follows by a recognizable local inference, DO use a precise
   marker such as “hence,” “clearly,” “note that,” “in particular,” or
   “moreover.” AVOID a marker when the inference still needs an argument.

7. WHEN an argument is long, DO reveal its joints: stages, cases, local
   claims, named obligations, and the exact result that closes each one.
   AVOID a continuous derivation whose purpose is visible only at the end.

8. WHEN prior work supplies a tool, DO cite the exact result at the point of
   use and state what it supplies. AVOID a detached chronological catalogue.

9. WHEN an assumption may look excessive, DO identify the line or lemma that
   uses it, or give the example that blocks its removal. AVOID defensive
   adjectives and vague assurances.

10. WHEN a paper is large, DO provide an annotated dependency map, audience
    route, or “used only in” notice. AVOID a roadmap that merely repeats
    section titles.

11. WHEN choosing paragraph boundaries, DO assign one mathematical job to
    each paragraph or theorem-like block. A dense one-sentence paragraph is
    acceptable if it performs one job. AVOID splitting or merging by length
    alone.

12. WHEN there is a genuine missing case, DO ask an exact question naming the
    cardinal, hypothesis, comparison, or preservation property. AVOID a broad
    sentence saying that further research would be interesting.

13. WHEN a general framework and a characteristic application coexist, DO
    distinguish them explicitly and say which parts are used here. AVOID
    presenting topic-local notation as universal infrastructure.

14. WHEN editing, DO retain the mathematical density but shorten the longest
    sentence tail, punctuate displays, and remove source-management residue.
    AVOID imitating roughness as if it were intellectual style.


2. MATURITY MODEL
=================

The sampled “early” period begins in 1989, when Shelah was already a mature
scholar. Treat it as corpus-early established practice, not career formation.

CORPUS-EARLY PRACTICE uses manual TeX, long multi-obligation sentences, large
private macro systems, frequent “without loss of generality,” and dense inline
mathematics. Retain its direct definition-to-theorem motion, typed alphabet,
two-stage constructions, and explicit inferential spine. Do not retain its
font machinery, raw display syntax, or avoidable syntactic overload.

TRANSITION PRACTICE increasingly uses `amsart`, semantic macros, theorem
taxonomies, labels, annotated contents, and short task-specific blocks. This
is chiefly a source and organization transition. It does not replace the
earlier mathematical identity.

RECENT SOLE-AUTHORED PRACTICE makes the namespace and dependency map especially
explicit. A structured parameter is defined once and its components are then
retrieved by systematic subscripts; long proofs use fine-grained clause
addresses. Sentences have a shorter upper tail, but local mathematical density
remains high.

RECENT COAUTHORED PRACTICE may be smoother, more literature-led, more
display-heavy, and less first-person. Use it when the current genre calls for
that surface, but do not let it displace the sole-authored decision system.

When evidence conflicts, rank it as follows:

    stable cross-period decision rule
    > recent sole-authored same-section practice
    > transition sole-authored practice retained recently
    > representative coauthored practice
    > old manual-source habit or one-paper surface.


3. GLOBAL WRITING PRINCIPLES
============================

PRINCIPLE A — Put the obstruction before the machinery.

WHEN a new construction is needed, DO state the natural attempt and the exact
property it fails to preserve. Then introduce the correction. AVOID calling a
method “new” before the need for it is mathematically visible.

PRINCIPLE B — Make every assumption spend itself.

WHEN stating a theorem, DO arrange assumptions so later text can point to the
clause that is used. AVOID a cloud of “standard hypotheses.” If a hypothesis
is only for one application, move it to that application.

PRINCIPLE C — Build an addressable proof.

WHEN a proof has several independent burdens, DO name them `(a)`, `(b)`,
`(*)_1`, or by short claims. AVOID inventing labels for trivial algebra. A
label earns its place by being cited or by clarifying the dependency graph.

PRINCIPLE D — Separate status from confidence.

WHEN a statement is proved, proposed, assumed, recalled, heuristic, or
numerical, DO mark that status with the correct environment and verb. AVOID
using “clearly” to turn a wish into a theorem or “perhaps” to weaken a proof.

PRINCIPLE E — Compress repeated structure, not logical responsibility.

WHEN a heterogeneous object recurs, DO define a tuple and systematic component
notation. WHEN a formula occurs once, write it directly if naming it adds
memory cost. AVOID both symbol proliferation and repeated opaque expressions.

PRINCIPLE F — Use local standard notation consciously.

WHEN the field has a strong convention, DO start from it. If the paper must
override it, declare the override in one sentence. AVOID silent switching
between standard and private spellings.

PRINCIPLE G — Treat the reader as a collaborator in a long proof.

WHEN a section is optional, preparatory, or used only once, DO say so and name
the destination. AVOID forcing every reader through every technical branch.

PRINCIPLE H — Let transitions certify relations.

WHEN using “hence,” “however,” “moreover,” or “in particular,” DO ensure the
next sentence respectively follows, contrasts, adds an independent fact, or
specializes. AVOID connective rotation for stylistic variety.

PRINCIPLE I — Locate limitations mathematically.

WHEN a result cannot be strengthened, DO show the counterexample, critical
case, or failed clause. AVOID generic “limitations” paragraphs detached from
the theorem.

PRINCIPLE J — Close on a changed mathematical state.

WHEN ending a proof or section, DO name the obtained bound, constructed
object, discharged clause, or remaining exact question. AVOID a generic recap
of activity.


4. MATHEMATICAL NOTATION
========================

This is the primary operational layer. Design notation as a typed, scoped
system. The examples are exact compact native-TeX specimens. `DIRECT` means
reuse is allowed for the same role; `ADAPT` means preserve the construction
while replacing topic-specific bases; `LOCAL_ONLY` means evidence or warning,
never a default.

4.1 Notation design principles
------------------------------

[N01] WHEN several object classes coexist, DO distinguish them by case and
font before adding indices: lower-case functions/elements/conditions, plain
capitals for individual sets or structures, calligraphic capitals for
families, blackboard capitals for forcing notions when useful, and fraktur for
a structured class or algebraic object. AVOID a decorative font change with
no type change. WHY: typography should reduce collision risk.

[N02] WHEN a relation depends on an ambient object, DO put that object on the
relation: `M\le_{\frak K}N`, `f<_Dg`, `p\le_{\mathbb Q}q`. AVOID repeatedly
explaining the ambient order in prose. WHY: a semantic relation remains
locally readable in a dense formula.

[N03] WHEN an alphabet has a global role, DO reserve it explicitly. AVOID
assuming that Greek always means cardinal or that `M` always means model. WHY:
the corpus deliberately reuses letters in different topics.

[N04] WHEN a notation needs more than two accents or indices to remain unique,
DO introduce a structured object or auxiliary definition. AVOID visual stacks
whose parsing cost exceeds the prose they replace.

4.2 Typed symbol palette
------------------------

Use the following as defaults only inside compatible mathematical domains:

- Cardinals and cardinal parameters: `\lambda,\kappa,\mu,\chi,\theta`.
- Ordinals and transfinite stages: `\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\varepsilon,
  \zeta,\xi`.
- Finite indices: `i,j,k,\ell,m,n,r,s`, after declaring their range.
- Models/structures: `M,N`; theory: `T`; class: `K`; structured class:
  `\frak K`.
- Functions/maps: `f,g,h`; forcing conditions: `p,q`; filters/ideals: `D,J`.
- Forcing notions: `\mathbb P,\mathbb Q` or locally defined `\bbP,\bbQ`.
- Sequences: a barred base together with an angle-bracket declaration.

WHEN a topic convention conflicts with this palette, DO keep the topic
convention and state the local reservation. AVOID forcing set-theory meanings
onto probability, algebra, or finite combinatorics.

4.3 Stable and conditional concept/role mappings
------------------------------------------------

[N05] WHEN defining a size stratum of a class, DO attach the cardinal below
the class symbol. EXAMPLE: `K_\lambda`. WHEN combining the class and its
relation, DO package them once as a structured object.

[N06] WHEN naming a family indexed by a stage or node, DO use a subscript and
declare the range in an angle-bracket family. WHEN two parallel structures
are compared, DO place the side/variant in a superscript and the stage below.

[N07] WHEN writing forcing, DO distinguish the forcing notion, conditions,
generic names, and ground-model parameters typographically. Declare which
direction of `\le` means “stronger.” This rule is mandatory because the
corpus contains both orientations.

[N08] WHEN a filter, ideal, model class, or order controls a relation, DO use a
semantic subscript rather than a new verbal abbreviation. DEFINE positive
parts, duals, bounded ideals, and quotient orders before chaining them.

4.4 Modifier grammar
--------------------

Use modifiers compositionally:

    base object + subscript       -> member, stage, coordinate, or parameter
    base object + superscript     -> owner, side, version, or operation
    base object + bar             -> declared sequence/family, occasionally extension
    base object + dot/name mark   -> forcing name
    base object + star            -> explicitly distinguished local variant
    cardinal + plus signs         -> successor iteration

[N09] WHEN a bar denotes a sequence, DO give its angle-bracket expansion at
first use. WHEN a bar denotes extension or closure instead, DO define it by an
equation. AVOID treating the accent as self-explanatory.

[N10] WHEN a superscript denotes ownership, DO use a form such as `f^p` and
keep it distinct from algebraic powers. WHEN ambiguity remains, use a tuple
projection phrase in prose.

[N11] WHEN a star marks a positive part, maximal object, or exceptional case,
DO state which. AVOID introducing `A_*`, `p_*`, and `q_*` in one local scope
unless their roles are visibly parallel.

4.5 Index grammar
-----------------

[N12] WHEN declaring a sequence, DO use `\langle X_i:i<\alpha\rangle` or the
domain-appropriate equivalent. The colon separates the object expression from
its index range.

[N13] WHEN an object varies by side and stage, DO keep the order stable, as in
`I^\ell_n`; do not alternate among subscript orderings, superscript roles, and
prose.

[N14] WHEN fixed and varying indices coexist, DO explain their roles and keep
fixed structural parameters farther from the base only if that convention is
consistent throughout. AVOID guessing index roles from letters.

[N15] WHEN nested clauses are needed, DO use a stable hierarchy such as
`1) -> (a) -> (\alpha) -> (*)_1`. Cite the smallest address that contains the
needed assertion.

4.6 Operator, relation, and delimiter conventions
-------------------------------------------------

[N16] DO romanize named operators and abbreviations with portable LaTeX:
`\operatorname{dom}`, `\operatorname{cf}`, `\operatorname{rk}`, or a declared
operator. AVOID private font tricks and unspaced text inside math.

[N17] DO use angle brackets for ordered sequences and braces for sets. In set
comprehension, separate the object from its condition with a colon or vertical
bar chosen consistently. Use parentheses for tuples and function arguments.

[N18] DO attach forcing/model/order context directly to `\Vdash`, `\models`,
`\le`, or `<`. AVOID one bare order symbol for several simultaneous orders.

[N19] DO size delimiters only when nesting requires it. AVOID automatic
`\left...\right` around every short expression.

4.7 Namespace, scope, reuse, and collision rules
------------------------------------------------

Global reservations live in a Notation block; specialized structures may be
section-global; witnesses and obligation markers are theorem- or proof-local.
Reuse a base only after its scope closes. After a long gap, recall the defining
label rather than silently redefine the symbol.

Known collision controls:

- `M`: model in model theory, finite order/domain in combinatorics.
- `T`: theory, tree, torus, or type parameter.
- `p`: forcing condition, prime/parameter, or probability.
- `D,J`: filter, ideal, or ultrafilter.
- `\alpha`: ordinal in the dominant corpus, probability exponent in a local
  modern paper.
- `\xi`: ordinal in several papers, explicitly non-ordinal in one recent
  finite-combinatorial paper.
- bar: sequence/family or extension/closure.
- `\le`: opposite forcing-strength conventions occur.

WHEN two meanings could be active together, DO rename, add a semantic font or
subscript, or state a local exception. AVOID trusting disciplinary habit alone.

4.8 Notation introduction and lifecycle
---------------------------------------

Use this sequence:

    ambient assumptions
    -> object type and domain/range
    -> exact definition or tuple
    -> numbered properties/components
    -> one collision-preventing convention
    -> sustained use through stable labels
    -> specialization by index or modifier.

Use a consolidated block for global alphabets, orders, and repeated operators.
Introduce proof-local witnesses just in time. If an object disappears after
one line, prefer the expression itself unless naming it clarifies a later
reference.

4.9 Equation-composition rules
------------------------------

[N20] WHEN a display defines an object, DO prepare it with a sentence that
names the object type and follow it with the property that matters. AVOID
repeating the display word for word.

[N21] WHEN a definition contains several constraints, DO write the outer
tuple/equality first and then list the constraints. AVOID hiding the object's
shape at the end of the list.

[N22] WHEN a proof needs the same complex assertion several times, DO name it
with a local marker and cite that marker. AVOID permanent global notation for
a one-proof obligation.

[N23] WHEN the general formula obscures the mechanism, DO state a special case
or interpretation immediately after it. WHEN the generality is essential, DO
explain why the special case would not suffice.

[N24] WHEN a display is part of a sentence, DO punctuate it. Use `equation`,
`align`, `gather`, or `\[...\]` according to the logical shape; do not imitate
old raw `$$` usage.

4.11 Temporal, topic, and coauthor qualifications
-------------------------------------------------

Old source forms—manual font families, hand-built forcing glyphs, `$$`, and
private spacing commands—are historical implementations, not present-day
rules. Forcing and model-theory mappings have the strongest repeated support;
algebra, probability, and finite combinatorics require their own conventional
palettes. Smooth display-rich coauthored work demonstrates a viable venue
adaptation but does not erase the compact sole-authored namespace.

4.12 Notation DO / DO NOT checklist
-----------------------------------

DO declare global alphabets and order direction. DO type every object before
sustained use. DO give ranges for indices. DO use semantic relation subscripts.
DO define a bar, star, dot, or ownership superscript. DO make complex
definitions clause-addressable. DO keep one spelling for restriction, set
difference, forcing names, and operators.

DO NOT infer meaning from frequency. DO NOT export a private macro without its
definition. DO NOT assume `p\le q` has a universal forcing meaning. DO NOT use
one bare letter for two live roles. DO NOT stack modifiers to rescue a crowded
namespace. DO NOT reuse `LOCAL_ONLY` specimens as defaults.


5. PROSE–MATH INTERFACE
=======================

[PM1] WHEN introducing a formula, DO tell the reader its job before the
display: definition, target inequality, compatibility condition, induction
hypothesis, or obstruction. AVOID “we have” when a more exact verb is
available.

[PM2] WHEN a display introduces new symbols, DO define the object type and
index range either immediately before it or in its first clause. AVOID making
the reader reverse-engineer types from legal operations.

[PM3] WHEN a display completes an inference, DO state afterward which clause,
bound, or theorem it establishes. AVOID paraphrasing every symbol. Interpret
the logical payload, not the typography.

[PM4] WHEN a relation is nonstandard, DO read it once in prose: for example,
say which condition is stronger, what `<_D` means, or which kind of embedding
`\le_{\frak K}` represents. Thereafter use the notation without repeated
glosses.

[PM5] WHEN mathematics sits inside a sentence, DO preserve grammatical
continuity and punctuation. AVOID a paragraph that ends before an unexplained
display and restarts on an unrelated subject.

[PM6] WHEN a formula has several quantified conditions, DO move them into a
lettered list if prose would require more than one level of subordination.
AVOID compressing conditions merely to make the theorem occupy fewer lines.

[PM7] WHEN an intuitive gloss is available, DO place it after the formal
definition and name the feature it isolates. AVOID a metaphor that leaves the
formal role less clear. Intuition is a second representation, not a substitute.

[PM8] WHEN a calculation is routine but necessary, DO give the one equality
or inclusion that carries the conclusion and cite the prior clause. AVOID both
“obvious” with no witness and a full derivation of algebra that contributes no
idea.

Preferred local sequence:

    prose purpose
    -> typed notation
    -> formula or clause list
    -> exact consequence
    -> optional intuition or forward reference.


6. MATHEMATICAL NARRATIVE
=========================

Choose a narrative algorithm that matches the mathematical burden.

THEOREM FROM A KNOWN BOUNDARY

    canonical result or earlier theorem
    -> exact remaining case
    -> main theorem
    -> one sentence on method
    -> dependency map.

Use this when the contribution closes a named gap. State the old and new
assumptions in commensurable notation.

FAILED ANALOGUE AND REPAIR

    natural analogue
    -> property expected to be preserved
    -> precise failure
    -> corrected object/framework
    -> characteristic theorem
    -> wider versions or applications.

This is especially Shelah-like. Do not describe the repair until the reader
can see what is broken.

CONSTRUCTION IN STAGES

    target properties
    -> stage-one object
    -> stage-two killing/extension step
    -> preservation lemmas
    -> enumeration/exhaustion argument
    -> verification of the target.

At each stage, name the invariant that survives. End with the property that
was not automatic.

ABSTRACT FRAMEWORK TO INSTANCE

    Context/Convention
    -> typed structured parameter
    -> general definitions
    -> sufficient conditions
    -> main conclusion
    -> concrete instance
    -> optional wider framework.

Tell the reader which abstract clauses are actually used. Generality must
earn itself through more than renaming.

INDUCTION OR APPROXIMATION

    induction parameter
    -> approximation at stage n
    -> compatibility obligation
    -> extension at n+1
    -> limit object
    -> property inherited at the limit.

State why the stage data are enough for the next step. Do not hide the
induction invariant in the construction prose.

CONTRADICTION

    desired assertion
    -> assume a minimal/specific counterexample
    -> derive an object violating a named clause
    -> write the incompatible statements
    -> conclude the assertion.

Prefer “this contradicts clause (c)” or the false inequality over the stock
phrase “we get a contradiction.”

LIMITATION OR NON-IMPROVABILITY

    tempting strengthening
    -> witness/counterexample/critical scale
    -> exact clause that fails
    -> strongest statement still supported.

Keep this near the theorem it qualifies. A limitation is part of the result's
shape, not an afterthought.


7. VOCABULARY RULES
===================

Use vocabulary as a status-and-inference system.

PROVED RESULTS

- Use “we prove” for a theorem whose proof is supplied.
- Use “we show” for a conclusion, comparison, or property established here.
- Use “we construct” when the mathematical contribution is an object/model.
- Use “we conclude” only after the stated premises have been discharged.
- Use “it follows from [result]” when the cited implication is genuinely
  immediate after matching hypotheses.

PROPOSALS AND OPEN WORK

- Use “we conjecture” or a Conjecture environment for a definite proposition.
- Use “we ask whether” or a Question environment for an exact missing case.
- Use “we intend to consider” only for a concrete continuation, not as proof.
- Use “we may hope” for programmatic possibility; never let it sound proved.

HEURISTICS

- Use “the point is,” “the idea is,” or “this suggests” to explain mechanism.
- Use “it seems” only when the statement is genuinely evidential or
  provisional.
- Follow a heuristic with the theorem, example, or question it motivates.

ASSUMPTIONS AND CONTEXT

- Use “Assume …” in a theorem or local argument.
- Use “We work in …” for a section- or paper-wide foundation.
- Use “Suppose …” for a temporary branch or contradiction setup.
- Use “We may assume …” only after naming the reduction, symmetry, or earlier
  result that permits it.

CITED FACTS

- Write “By [specific result], …” when applying it now.
- Write “Recall that …” when the fact has already been introduced or is
  standard for the intended reader.
- Write “See [reference]” for background or omitted proof, not for the main
  logical step unless the exact result is named.

NUMERICAL, ASYMPTOTIC, OR PROBABILISTIC CLAIMS

- Distinguish equality, inequality, asymptotic order, convergence in
  probability, high probability, consistency, and existence.
- Put the parameter regime next to the conclusion.
- Do not strengthen “is consistent,” “with probability tending to one,” or
  “for sufficiently large” into an unconditional assertion.

INFERENTIAL CONNECTIVES

- “hence”: the next assertion follows from the preceding local facts.
- “clearly”: the verification is genuinely immediate for the intended reader;
  use sparingly and never across a hidden lemma.
- “note that”: draw attention to a fact needed later or a possible
  misreading.
- “in particular”: specialize a stronger statement already established.
- “moreover”: add an independent strengthening under the same setup.
- “however” / “on the other hand”: state a real contrast, limitation, or
  alternate direction.
- “indeed”: supply the verification of the immediately preceding assertion.

AVOID promotional adjectives such as “groundbreaking,” “remarkable,” or
“powerful” unless a precise comparison follows. AVOID near-synonym rotation.
Repeated exact words are better than blurred logical roles.


8. SENTENCE ARCHITECTURE
========================

The mature target is compact but not artificially simple. Aim for one logical
spine per sentence and keep the 90th-percentile tail under control.

Preferred architectures:

1. **Context -> exact claim.** “Assume [typed hypotheses]. Then [conclusion].”
2. **Known result -> missing case.** Attribute the theorem; name the parameter
   or hypothesis it does not cover.
3. **Obstacle -> repair.** State why the natural construction fails; make the
   new definition the main clause of the next sentence.
4. **Definition -> consequence.** Define the object in the main clause; use
   “hence” for the immediate property that will be used.
5. **Claim -> witness.** State the local claim; begin the next sentence with
   the object that verifies it.
6. **Condition -> choice -> purpose.** Use only one subordinate layer; if
   several choices are required, make a numbered list.
7. **Result -> qualification.** State the proved core first, then attach the
   exact boundary with “provided,” “when,” or “unless.”
8. **General case -> characteristic case.** Give the framework, then a short
   sentence identifying the case treated here.
9. **Long setup -> short landing.** A technical sentence may define all local
   data; follow it with “This proves (b)” or the exact resulting relation.
10. **Question -> program.** Ask the mathematical question, then state which
    construction or comparison would answer it.

Information placement rules:

- Put the object and action in the main clause; do not bury the theorem behind
  historical subordinate clauses.
- Keep quantifier scope next to the symbol it controls.
- Use parentheses for a compact clarification, alternate convention, or exact
  citation. If the parenthesis contains a second argument, promote it to a
  sentence or remark.
- A semicolon may join two mathematically parallel clauses. Do not use it to
  conceal a new rhetorical task.
- Prefer active plural agency for choices and constructions. Use passive voice
  when the actor is irrelevant and the mathematical object should remain the
  subject.
- Do not mimic article omissions, agreement errors, misspellings, or abrupt
  fragments found in draft sources.


9. RHETORICAL MOVE ALGORITHMS
==============================

ALGORITHM 1 — Introduction to a closure result

    prior theorem
    -> exact leftover cases
    -> why “surely true” is not proof
    -> strengthened property or construction
    -> theorem closing every case
    -> section map keyed to proof burdens.

ALGORITHM 2 — New framework

    desired application
    -> existing framework and its failure
    -> structured parameter
    -> definitions with addressable clauses
    -> main preservation/construction theorem
    -> application and wider variants.

ALGORITHM 3 — Comparison theorem

    objects to compare
    -> known inequalities/implications
    -> missing directions or consistency patterns
    -> complete list of possibilities
    -> references to the claims proving each case.

ALGORITHM 4 — Programmatic paper

    long-term problem
    -> result achieved here
    -> unresolved sharper target
    -> why this partial step adds information even under stronger axioms
    -> next precise question.

ALGORITHM 5 — Reviewer-facing boundary

    plausible objection or strengthening
    -> example showing failure
    -> exact hypothesis used in the proof
    -> revised maximal claim.

ALGORITHM 6 — Reader routing

    identify audience/prerequisite
    -> annotate each major section by what it proves
    -> mark optional branches and their destinations
    -> state where the main theorem is assembled.

Use these moves without theatrical setup. A “puzzle” is a concrete mismatch
between statements, not a marketing label.


10. PARAGRAPH ENGINES
=====================

Select one engine per paragraph or theorem-like block.

P1 — OBJECT DEFINITION

    object type -> exact notation -> clauses -> one operational consequence.

P2 — KNOWN BOUNDARY

    cited theorem -> covered regime -> uncovered regime -> current target.

P3 — OBSTRUCTION

    natural attempt -> failed property -> reason -> need for correction.

P4 — CONSTRUCTION STEP

    current stage -> chosen object -> preserved invariant -> next-stage access.

P5 — LOCAL CLAIM

    claim -> short proof/witness -> exact role in the parent proof.

P6 — CASE

    case hypothesis -> specialized simplification -> deduction -> discharged
    parent clause.

P7 — COMPARISON

    first formulation -> second formulation -> implication each way or exact
    failure of one direction.

P8 — LIMITATION

    tempting stronger claim -> counterexample/critical case -> corrected
    boundary.

P9 — APPLICATION

    previously proved framework theorem -> verification of its hypotheses ->
    obtained domain result.

P10 — NOTATION RECALL

    cited definition -> only the components needed here -> local specialization.

P11 — READER ROUTE

    section's job -> dependencies -> optionality -> destination result.

P12 — OPEN QUESTION

    established fact -> remaining parameter/hypothesis -> exact question ->
    why the present method stops.

P13 — PROOF CLOSURE

    last required clause -> inference -> named theorem/claim completed.

P14 — SECTION CLOSURE

    section output -> where it is used next -> no generic recap.

Opening rule: begin with the paragraph's object or logical job, not a vague
pronoun. Closing rule: land on a consequence, dependency, or boundary. Keep
citations clustered around the result they support. Put qualifications beside
the claim they limit, not in an apologetic tail several paragraphs later.


11. SECTION MODELS
==================

INTRODUCTION

Purpose: identify the exact mathematical pressure and state what the paper
does about it.

Opening behavior: begin with a known theorem, a natural construction, a named
object, or a preservation/comparison question. Move quickly to the boundary.

Typical sequence:

    known setting
    -> exact obstruction or uncovered case
    -> principal claim/question
    -> core device
    -> limitations or characteristic case
    -> dependency-aware roadmap.

Paragraph policy: use separate paragraphs for the known boundary, obstruction,
repair, theorem, and roadmap. Claim strength is direct for proved results and
explicitly provisional for programs. Math density may be high, but introduce
only the notation needed to state the problem. Cite exact antecedent results at
the point of comparison. Close by telling the reader where the main theorem is
proved and which sections are optional. Avoid broad field importance,
inflated novelty, and a roadmap that merely lists titles.

LITERATURE REVIEW / PRIOR WORK

Purpose: expose the dependency and the unresolved mathematical relation, not
provide a census of publications.

Opening behavior: name the result, method, or line of work that the current
argument actually uses. Organize by implication, technique, or boundary rather
than chronology.

Typical sequence:

    cited result and hypotheses
    -> what it supplies
    -> where it stops
    -> relation to the current theorem
    -> any competing direction or example.

Keep citations adjacent to their logical function. Separate “continued in,”
“generalized by,” and “used here” because they make different claims. A
standalone literature section is optional; in technical mathematics, integrate
prior work into the introduction and the first point of use. Avoid priority
claims not needed for the argument and vague clusters of references.

THEORY / DEFINITIONS / FRAMEWORK

Purpose: create an addressable universe in which the theorem can be stated and
proved.

Opening behavior: give Context or Convention, then the global alphabet and
ambient assumptions. Define the outer structured object before its components.

Typical sequence:

    ambient foundation and fixed parameters
    -> notation and order conventions
    -> main object as tuple/class
    -> numbered component conditions
    -> examples or sufficient conditions
    -> elementary observations/claims
    -> main framework theorem.

Use high math density, short prose bridges, and many local labels. Every new
symbol must have an object type and scope. State whether a definition is
standard, modified, or specific to this paper. A remark should clarify a
collision, boundary, or later use. Close by listing exactly which framework
clauses the next theorem needs. Avoid defining an entire private vocabulary
before any mathematical need is visible.

METHODS / CONSTRUCTION

Purpose: explain how the target object, model, forcing, or probabilistic
estimate is produced and why required properties survive.

Opening behavior: restate the target properties and divide the method into
stages or invariants.

Typical sequence:

    construction data
    -> stage rule
    -> local extension/existence lemma
    -> preservation argument
    -> limit or generic object
    -> verification against the target list.

For a computational or probabilistic method, replace “generic object” with the
sample/model/algorithm, but retain the invariant ledger. Defend every choice
that is not standard: support, ordering, threshold, completeness, or index
range. Use displays for the defining rule and critical bound; use prose for
why they work. Close by matching obtained properties to the theorem clauses.
Avoid a chronological log of actions with no invariant.

RESULTS

Purpose: state what has been proved under exactly which conditions, and connect
it to the question that generated it.

Opening behavior: give the strongest clean theorem early. If several outcomes
form a classification, state the cases in parallel notation.

Typical sequence:

    theorem with assumptions
    -> immediate corollary
    -> comparison with previous bound/result
    -> exceptional or critical case
    -> example showing sharpness/non-improvability
    -> application.

In pure mathematics the Results section may be distributed through theorem
sections rather than named separately. Claim strength must distinguish
existence, consistency, implication, equivalence, upper/lower bounds, and
independence. In empirical/numerical work, distinguish computed observation
from proved fact and report the regime, tolerance, or probability model.
Citations explain comparison, not proof of the new theorem. Close with the
precise consequence used later. Avoid “supports” or “demonstrates” when a
formal relation is available.

DISCUSSION / CONCLUSION

Purpose: identify what the theorem changes, where the method stops, and the
next exact mathematical question.

Opening behavior: begin with the strongest conceptual consequence, not a full
abstract repeated in past tense.

Typical sequence:

    result's changed boundary
    -> relation to the broader program
    -> assumption or direction not settled
    -> exact question/conjecture
    -> possible method only if concrete.

Keep the section short in a theorem paper. It may be replaced by a Conclusion,
Question, Discussion, or final application block. Use no new global notation.
Calibrate possibilities with “we ask,” “we conjecture,” “it may be possible,”
or “the present proof does not.” Avoid promotional summary and vague calls for
future research.

PROOF SECTION

Purpose: discharge an addressable list of obligations while keeping the main
idea visible.

Opening behavior: state the proof plan and isolate the bottleneck. If the proof
is immediate from earlier results, name the results and match their hypotheses.

Typical sequence:

    setup and fixed notation
    -> claim/invariant
    -> cases or induction
    -> local calculations
    -> assembly
    -> explicit closure of theorem.

Use local labels only when referenced. Begin each case by stating its
hypothesis; end it by naming the discharged clause. Put long routine
verification after the conceptual step, not before it. Avoid unexplained
“clearly,” silent changes of convention, and endings that do not say what has
been proved.

APPENDIX

Purpose: hold self-contained technical material, absoluteness, background, or
verification whose inclusion in the main line would hide the mechanism.

Opening behavior: say exactly which main-text claims depend on the appendix
and whether it can be read independently. Preserve the main namespace unless a
fresh Context explicitly resets it.

Typical sequence:

    dependency notice
    -> recalled assumptions
    -> technical lemma or alternate proof
    -> cross-reference back to each consumer.

Do not exile a central idea merely because it is difficult. Move detail when
the main text can state the operative lemma and its role without deception.


12. FLOW AND TRANSITION RULES
==============================

Flow is primarily conceptual continuity plus exact references; connectives are
secondary labels on that structure.

SENTENCE TO SENTENCE

- Carry the main object from old information to new information.
- Use “Indeed” to verify the preceding claim, “Hence” to state its consequence,
  “However” to restrict or oppose it, and “In particular” to specialize it.
- Begin with a precise noun or symbol after a dense sentence; avoid a chain of
  “this,” “it,” and “the latter” with multiple candidates.

PARAGRAPH TO PARAGRAPH

- End the first paragraph with the unresolved obligation; begin the next with
  the definition, lemma, or case that discharges it.
- A bridge sentence should name both sides: “To prove clause (c), we now define
  …” is better than “We next continue the proof.”
- After a long local proof, restate the obtained property, not the entire setup.

SECTION TO SECTION

- End with the section's output and its next consumer.
- Open the next section by recalling only the required object and label.
- In a long paper, annotated contents may state these edges globally; local
  transitions must still work without consulting the map.

Avoid mechanical alternation among “thus,” “therefore,” and “hence.” Avoid
transition phrases that do not encode a logical edge. Repetition of a key
technical noun is preferable to an ambiguous synonym.


13. LATEX CODING STYLE
======================

DOCUMENT ARCHITECTURE

- Use a current mathematical class, normally `amsart` unless the venue
  supplies another class.
- Use `\section` and `\subsection` semantically. Number the Introduction as §0
  only if the project consistently uses that convention; otherwise follow the
  venue.
- For a long paper, add an annotated contents block only when it records jobs,
  dependencies, optional sections, or label ranges.

THEOREM ENVIRONMENTS

- Define a compact taxonomy actually used by the paper: theorem, proposition,
  lemma, claim, corollary, definition, notation, convention, context, remark,
  example, question, conjecture, discussion, and conclusion as needed.
- Share counters when that improves reference order. Do not declare dozens of
  near-synonyms that occur once.
- Put proof status in the environment; do not simulate theorem heads with bold
  text and spacing commands.

MACROS

- Prefer semantic macros (`\restr`, `\dom`, `\cf`, a recurring forcing or
  class) over formatting macros.
- Use `\DeclareMathOperator` or `\operatorname` for named operators.
- Define every private macro in the source delivered to the reader. Never
  assume a personal file such as a font or bibliography helper is available.
- Avoid exporting historical macros such as `\mn`, `\sn`, `\bn`, hand-built
  forcing glyphs, or one-letter font factories.
- A macro such as `\cA` is safe only within the current source; its expansion
  has varied across papers.

MATH TYPESETTING

- Use inline math for short typed assertions and structured displays for
  definitions, pivotal inequalities, case systems, and aligned derivations.
- Replace raw `$$` with `\[...\]`, `equation`, `align`, or `gather`.
- Punctuate displays as parts of sentences.
- Avoid indiscriminate delimiter sizing and manual negative spacing.

LABELS AND REFERENCES

- Give labels a readable local scheme such as `def:frame`, `clm:preserve`, or
  `thm:main`; short internal codes are acceptable in a long established
  project only if a dependency map makes them usable.
- Cite the smallest address needed: theorem, clause, or equation.
- Use `\eqref` for equations and `\ref`/the venue's smart reference command for
  structural units. Keep capitalization consistent.

CITATIONS, FIGURES, AND TABLES

- Place citations after the exact result or method attributed.
- Introduce a figure/table before it appears and state the mathematical fact it
  makes visible afterward.
- Do not infer a figure/table style from this largely theorem-driven corpus;
  follow the current venue and subject.


14. CLAIM-STRENGTH CALIBRATION
==============================

Every important statement must occupy one of these lanes.

PROVED: use a theorem-like environment or “we prove/show/construct.” State all
quantifiers and assumptions. “Clearly” may shorten only an immediate
verification inside the proof.

PROPOSED: use Definition for a new object, Conjecture for a definite unproved
proposition, and Question for an exact open case. “We propose” does not imply
existence or correctness.

HEURISTIC: use “the point is,” “this suggests,” or “it seems” and identify the
formal result the heuristic motivates. Do not cite a heuristic later as a
lemma.

ASSUMED: use “Assume,” “Suppose,” “We work in,” or a Context/Hypothesis block.
Distinguish global foundation, theorem hypothesis, and temporary case.

CITED: name the author/result or exact reference and say whether it supplies a
definition, implication, construction, or consistency statement. Check that
the cited hypotheses match the present ones.

NUMERICAL/COMPUTATIONAL: state that a value was computed, simulated, or
observed; give parameters and uncertainty. Do not write “we prove” unless a
formal proof accompanies it.

CONSISTENCY/INDEPENDENCE: preserve the modality. “It is consistent that P” is
not “P”; “P is not provable from ZFC” is not “not P”; an implication under a
large-cardinal assumption is not an unconditional theorem.

Before finalizing a claim, ask: What is its status? What are its quantifiers?
Which assumptions does it spend? What exact sentence would falsify an
overstatement?


15. REVIEWER-DEFENSE BEHAVIOR
==============================

Anticipate objections at the mathematical joint they threaten.

1. If a definition looks stronger than necessary, identify the later clause
   that uses the strength or state the weaker unresolved version.
2. If a natural alternative construction exists, explain the precise
   preservation, support, compactness, or complexity property it loses.
3. If a theorem resembles prior work, put both statements in the same notation
   and isolate the new range, direction, or conclusion.
4. If a proof says “without loss,” name the symmetry or reduction.
5. If a result may appear obvious from many special cases, say why that is not
   a proof and identify the case requiring the new idea.
6. If a long framework is used only once, route readers to the characteristic
   case and justify retaining the general version.
7. If an assumption cannot be removed, put the counterexample or critical
   scale near the theorem.
8. If an earlier version contained an error, state the corrected mathematical
   claim and dependency concisely; omit source-management conversation.

The tone is factual. Do not plead, over-apologize, or claim robustness without
a theorem, example, or check.


16. DO / DO NOT
===============

DO begin with the mathematical object or obstruction. DO state the main result
early. DO declare the alphabet, scope, and order convention. DO make complex
definitions and proofs addressable. DO use direct verbs for proved claims. DO
use first-person plural for mathematical choices. DO expose stages, cases, and
invariants. DO cite dependencies at first use. DO place limitations beside the
affected claim. DO provide reader routes in long work. DO end blocks with a
consequence or exact question. DO modernize the TeX while preserving semantic
macros.

DO NOT copy source sentences. DO NOT imitate errors, fragments, or typist
notes. DO NOT begin with ceremonial importance. DO NOT assign a universal
meaning to a frequent symbol. DO NOT import topic-local macros. DO NOT leave
forcing order orientation implicit. DO NOT call an unproved expectation
“clear.” DO NOT use “without loss” without a reduction. DO NOT scatter
qualifications far from their scope. DO NOT let a coauthor-polished paper erase
the compact solo identity. DO NOT make annotated contents for a short linear
paper. DO NOT use raw `$$`, manual font internals, or layout-only macros.


17. SECTION-SPECIFIC CHECKLIST
==============================

INTRODUCTION

- Is the object/problem visible in the first paragraph?
- Is the exact obstruction or missing case stated before the machinery?
- Is the principal theorem/question present early with correct modality?
- Does the roadmap record mathematical jobs and dependencies?

PRIOR WORK

- Does every citation have a role: source, tool, comparison, or continuation?
- Are old and new assumptions expressed commensurably?
- Is novelty separated from provenance?

DEFINITIONS/THEORY

- Are foundation, global parameters, and order conventions explicit?
- Is every long definition clause-addressable?
- Are symbol types, domains, ranges, and scopes recoverable at first use?
- Does each remark solve an ambiguity or prepare later work?

METHOD/CONSTRUCTION

- Are target properties and invariants listed?
- Is each stage rule followed by its preservation reason?
- Are limit, generic, or termination steps justified?
- Does the final verification match every target clause?

RESULTS

- Are theorem assumptions and quantifiers complete?
- Are consistency, implication, equality, asymptotics, and computation kept
  distinct?
- Is sharpness or non-improvability located near the theorem?

PROOF

- Is the bottleneck named?
- Does every case state its hypothesis and discharged obligation?
- Are local labels reused rather than ornamental?
- Does the final sentence say exactly what has been proved?

DISCUSSION/CONCLUSION

- Does it state the changed boundary rather than repeat the abstract?
- Is every open direction an exact question or conjecture?
- Is any limitation tied to a theorem clause or example?

APPENDIX/LATEX

- Are appendix dependencies announced?
- Is notation preserved or explicitly reset?
- Are semantic macros portable and all dependencies included?
- Are displays punctuated, labels stable, and obsolete source tricks removed?


18. SELF-REVISION PROTOCOL
==========================

PASS 1 — STATUS

Mark every major statement as proved, proposed, heuristic, assumed, cited, or
numerical. Repair any sentence whose verb implies a stronger lane than its
evidence.

PASS 2 — MATHEMATICAL SKELETON

Write the dependency graph in one line:

    problem -> obstruction -> definitions -> local obligations -> main result
    -> limitation/application.

If a section or paragraph has no node or edge in this graph, justify it or
remove it.

PASS 3 — NOTATION

For every global symbol, record object class, meaning, scope, modifier roles,
and index ranges. Search for collisions in both directions: one symbol with
two meanings, and one meaning with two spellings. Verify forcing orientation,
relation subscripts, bars, stars, and name marks. Replace every unexplained
private macro.

PASS 4 — DEFINITIONS AND THEOREMS

Check that complex objects reveal their outer form first. Split long hypotheses
into addressable clauses. Confirm that later references point to the smallest
correct clause and that no assumption is unused or silently strengthened.

PASS 5 — PROSE–MATH INTERFACE

Read the sentence before and after each display. The first must state the
display's job; the second must state its consequence or interpretation when
one is needed. Add punctuation and remove verbal duplication.

PASS 6 — SENTENCES AND PARAGRAPHS

Underline the logical spine of every long sentence. If two independent actions
remain, split it or use a clause list. Label every paragraph with one engine
from Section 10. Split paragraphs with two jobs; merge fragments that perform
only half a job.

PASS 7 — FLOW

Audit every “hence,” “clearly,” “note that,” “in particular,” “moreover,” and
“however.” State the exact relation it claims. Delete it if no such relation
exists; add the missing argument if the relation is nontrivial.

PASS 8 — ATTRIBUTION AND DEFENSE

For every cited or compared result, verify provenance and hypotheses. Move
counterexamples and limitations next to the claim they constrain. Replace
“without loss of generality” and “for simplicity” with the actual reduction or
scope statement.

PASS 9 — MATURITY FILTER

Remove manual TeX idioms, raw display delimiters, font machinery, revision
comments, typist notes, vague contradiction formulas, and accidental
grammatical roughness. Retain compactness, direct status, local notation
discipline, proof addresses, and dependency maps.

PASS 10 — STANDALONE TEST

Give the draft to a mathematically qualified reader with no access to private
macros or project history. They must be able to identify the main theorem,
parse every symbol, follow the proof obligations, distinguish known from new,
and state the exact remaining question. If they cannot, revise the decision
system—not merely the wording.

FINAL TEST

The draft should feel like an argument under active control: dense because the
mathematics is dense, direct because claim status is explicit, and navigable
because every object and obligation has an address. If it is merely terse,
rough, or full of “clearly,” it has copied a surface and missed the style.
