AUTHOR STYLE SKILL
==================

Purpose
-------

Use this file to draft, revise, or evaluate mathematical writing in a style distilled from the available Grigori Perelman corpus. Treat the style as a system of decisions, not as a bank of sentences. The central qualities are direct entry into the mathematics, visible proof state, dependency-ordered exposition, compact local paragraphs, and unusual candor about assumptions, gaps, corrections, and goals not yet achieved.

The evidence supports a stable cross-period identity and a provisional recent native-TeX model. It does not support a definitive claim about a fully mature career style. Prefer the stable rules below, then the practices shared by the three high-fidelity 2002-2003 manuscripts. Use early PDF-derived evidence only for prose and rhetorical structure. Never infer notation, equation form, punctuation, or LaTeX practice from a PDF-derived transcription.

Before writing, identify five things: the mathematical object under discussion, the exact local task, the status of every claim, the dependencies needed for the next inference, and the point at which the paragraph or section will have completed its job. Preserve the supplied mathematics. If a proof is incomplete, make the incompleteness visible. If a numerical result has not been supplied, do not create a value, rate, table, or qualitative outcome. If a reference has not been supplied or verified, insert a clearly marked citation need rather than inventing one.


1. STYLE IDENTITY
=================

Retain the following features as the high-confidence identity of the style.

1. Enter through an exact object or question. Name the equation, manifold, operator, space, discretization, estimate, algorithm, theorem, or unresolved implication before discussing general importance.

2. Reach the operative claim early. In a short note, state the theorem or construction almost immediately. In a long paper, give only the prior results and obstruction needed to make the new claim intelligible.

3. Make the proof state visible. Tell the reader whether a statement is proved, assumed, proposed, heuristic, cited, observed numerically, deferred, or presently unjustified.

4. Organize exposition by logical dependence. Introduce an object before using it, state the condition before the conclusion it controls, and prove the local estimate before invoking it in a global argument.

5. Advance proofs through explicit local moves. Typical moves are: choose an object, normalize it, define an auxiliary quantity, derive an estimate, divide into cases, pass to a limit, invoke a named result, and state the consequence.

6. Use short paragraphs for long reasoning. A paragraph should normally perform one proof task, even when one or two sentences must be long enough to keep hypotheses and conclusions visibly connected.

7. Prefer mathematical verbs over promotional language. Use define, consider, construct, estimate, compare, prove, deduce, and conclude. Avoid vague announcements of novelty or importance.

8. State the obstruction rather than saying that a problem is difficult. Name the missing bound, failed compactness step, irregular object, nonuniform constant, unjustified passage, or unavailable theorem.

9. Expose causal and contrastive logic. Use consequence markers when a conclusion is earned, contrast markers when two cases or mechanisms differ, and no connective when the shared mathematical object already carries the transition.

10. Integrate prior work at the point of use. A citation should provide a theorem, locate a previous construction, identify an unresolved claim, or delimit the present contribution.

11. Close a proof when the advertised mathematical task is complete. Do not append a ceremonial paragraph merely to make the ending sound grand.

12. Be candid under review. If an earlier argument has a gap, say which step is unsupported and whether later conclusions depend on it. If a result is deferred, name the boundary of the current paper.

13. Use intuition only with a status label. A conceptual calculation may precede a rigorous proof, but the simplifying assumption and the later repair must be explicit.

14. Keep notation operational. Introduce symbols because the next definition, estimate, or construction needs them; avoid a detached catalogue of one-use symbols.

15. Let the mathematics supply the authority. Avoid ornamental prose, exaggerated adjectives, generic field surveys, and claims of breadth that are not embodied in a theorem or method.

3. GLOBAL WRITING PRINCIPLES
============================

RULE 1 - Object before context
WHEN opening a paper or section, DO name the mathematical object and what must be established about it. AVOID a generic opening about the importance, ubiquity, or rapid growth of the field. WHY: the style creates interest through a concrete mathematical tension.

RULE 2 - Obstruction before contribution
WHEN presenting new work, DO identify the exact failure in the existing route and then state what removes or bypasses it. AVOID announcing a “novel framework” before the reader knows what it must accomplish. WHY: the contribution receives its meaning from the obstruction.

RULE 3 - Status before elegance
WHEN a sentence could sound smoother by suppressing an assumption, proof gap, or source of evidence, DO keep the status visible. AVOID strengthening a verb or deleting a condition for rhythm. WHY: mathematical auditability takes priority over surface polish.

RULE 4 - Dependency before chronology
WHEN ordering material, DO follow the chain required by the proof or algorithm. AVOID narrating the order in which the author discovered the ideas unless that history is mathematically necessary. WHY: the reader needs the shortest dependable path to the conclusion.

RULE 5 - One paragraph, one mathematical job
WHEN a paragraph begins a logically independent task, DO break it. AVOID combining background, a new definition, a theorem, a numerical interpretation, and a limitation in one block. WHY: short local units expose the proof state.

RULE 6 - Conditions next to consequences
WHEN an estimate or theorem depends on regularity, geometry, discretization, parameter size, mesh conditions, or a cited result, DO state the dependency immediately before or inside the claim. AVOID distant qualifications that force the reader to reconstruct scope.

RULE 7 - Computation followed by function
WHEN a calculation produces a bound, sign, monotonicity property, contradiction, or compactness input, DO name that function in the next clause or sentence. AVOID leaving a display without saying why it was computed.

RULE 8 - Direct comparison
WHEN comparing with prior work, DO specify the differing hypothesis, conclusion, mechanism, or proof requirement. AVOID “stronger,” “better,” “more general,” or “simpler” without a precise axis.

RULE 9 - Local citation
WHEN a previous theorem or construction is used, DO cite it at the dependency point and state what it supplies. AVOID a detached list of related papers.

RULE 10 - Honest compression
WHEN an inference is genuinely immediate for the intended reader, DO compress it and, if helpful, use “clearly” or “it is easy to see.” WHEN the inference hides a case, estimate, or assumption, DO show that step. WHY: compression is permitted only after the proof obligation is small.

RULE 11 - Functional ending
WHEN the theorem, construction, or estimate is complete, DO end the local unit with the exact consequence. AVOID restating the entire paper in broader language. WHY: proof completion is itself a strong ending.

RULE 12 - Minimal scaffolding
WHEN a heading, notation block, macro, or transition does not help the reader locate a dependency, DO remove it. AVOID formal structure added only for visual fullness.


4. MATHEMATICAL NOTATION
========================

Only the recent native TeX supports this section. Treat the following as a flexible policy, not as a dictionary assigning fixed meanings to letters.

4.1 Local introduction

WHEN an object first enters an argument, DO introduce it in a complete local move: specify the object, its domain or ambient space when needed, the notation, and the property used next. Suitable architectures are “Let X denote ...,” “Consider ...,” “For ... define ...,” and “We denote by ...”. AVOID using an unexplained symbol in a theorem or display.

WHEN several quantities form one dependent system, DO introduce them in dependency order. For example: primary object -> scale or parameter -> auxiliary set or functional -> estimate to be proved. AVOID alphabetic notation lists whose order has no mathematical meaning.

4.2 Semantic discipline

Use conventional letters when their role is clear in the field, but do not infer a universal Perelman meaning for any symbol. WHEN a hat, bar, tilde, star, prime, or subscript is introduced, DO state the local operation or role. AVOID relying on typography alone to communicate projection, rescaling, time dependence, approximation, or duality.

WHEN an index appears, DO make its range and function recoverable: spatial coordinate, sequence number, iteration count, mesh level, component, or time step. AVOID stacking indices that force the reader to search backward for each meaning.

4.3 Definitions and equality

Use `:=` or an equivalent definition marker only when creating notation. Use `=` for an identity or derived equality and an inequality symbol for an estimate. WHEN a constant may change from line to line, DO declare that convention once and specify the quantities on which it may depend. In numerical analysis, state explicitly whether a constant is independent of the mesh size, polynomial order, time step, regularization parameter, or physical coefficient whenever that independence is part of the claim.

4.4 Auxiliary quantities

WHEN an auxiliary object shortens several later estimates, isolates a compactness argument, or makes a monotonicity mechanism visible, DO define it. AVOID one-use abbreviations. WHY: notation should compress recurring structure rather than conceal a single complicated line.

4.5 Scaling and normalization

WHEN a proof changes scale, norm, coordinates, or time variables, DO state the factor and the property preserved. Follow with the quantity that becomes normalized and the conclusion enabled by that normalization. AVOID saying merely “after rescaling” when the scale affects the scope of the theorem.

4.6 Numerical-analysis adaptation

WHEN exact and discrete objects coexist, DO choose related notation that exposes the relation and define every modifier. State the function spaces, mesh family, norm, and parameter regime required by a bound. Do not invent a conventional finite-element symbol if the source text uses another declared system. The author's mathematics always overrides the style default.


5. PROSE-MATH INTERFACE
=======================

5.1 Preparing a display

Before a display, state the task it performs: define a quantity, record an evolution law, combine estimates, choose a parameter, or express the contradiction. A short lead-in is enough. Avoid a display that arrives without a grammatical or logical role.

WHEN the display contains new symbols, define them first or immediately afterward before another inference depends on them. WHEN the display is a direct specialization of a previous formula, name the substitution or limiting regime.

5.2 Following a display

After a display, state the consequence that advances the argument. Preferred functions include:

- the right-hand side has a required sign;
- the quantity is monotone or uniformly bounded;
- the estimate contradicts the assumed extremal behavior;
- the bound supplies compactness or convergence;
- the chosen parameter makes two boundary conditions compatible;
- the numerical observation agrees with a stated rate on the tested data.

Do not translate every symbol into prose. Explain the mathematical function of the display.

5.3 Equation chains

WHEN several equalities or inequalities form one deduction, DO keep them together and identify the premise used at each nonobvious step. WHEN two displays serve different goals, separate them with prose. AVOID a page of formulas whose final target is not announced.

5.4 Intuition and rigor

WHEN a simplified calculation reveals the mechanism, DO label it as an idea, formal computation, heuristic, or argument under temporary assumptions. Then state the obstruction to making it rigorous and point to the later lemma, regularization, approximation, or compactness step that repairs it. AVOID presenting the sketch and the rigorous proof as if they had the same status.

5.5 Numerical evidence

Introduce a table or figure by the question it tests. State the exact computed quantity, parameter range, and comparison supplied by the input. Afterward, use “we observe,” “the experiment illustrates,” or “the computed values are consistent with” unless a separate theorem proves the conclusion. Never promote an observed slope or finite sample to a proved asymptotic rate.

5.6 Punctuation and typesetting

Treat a display as part of the logical sentence, but use modern readable punctuation consistently. The archival native sources often omit display punctuation; this is not a rule to reproduce. Mathematical clarity and reliable compilation take priority over copying source irregularities.


6. MATHEMATICAL NARRATIVE
=========================

The characteristic narrative is not a fixed theorem-lemma-corollary template. It is a sequence of local obligations whose completion can be checked.

6.1 Default proof narrative

Use the following sequence when it fits the mathematics:

1. State the target and all governing hypotheses.
2. Choose or normalize the central object.
3. Introduce only the auxiliary quantity needed for the next step.
4. Derive the estimate, monotonicity formula, comparison, or local structure.
5. Split into cases if distinct mechanisms apply.
6. Invoke a prior result at the exact point where its conclusion is needed.
7. State whether the step yields a bound, convergence, classification, or contradiction.
8. Return to the original target and close the proof.

6.2 Construction narrative

WHEN proving existence by construction, DO state the properties the object must satisfy, decompose it into building blocks if needed, and choose parameters in dependency order. Use the pattern:

required properties -> provisional ansatz -> constraints -> first parameter choice -> estimates uniform in later choices -> remaining parameter choices -> compatibility or smoothing -> final verification.

Avoid choosing all constants at once. Make quantifier order visible.

6.3 Compactness or limiting narrative

WHEN passing to a limit, DO state the normalization, the uniform estimates, the compactness theorem or extraction principle, the domain on which convergence holds, and the property inherited by the limit. If the limit cannot yet be extended to the required range, state that as the next local task. Do not write “taking a subsequence” without the bound that makes extraction legitimate.

6.4 Contradiction narrative

WHEN arguing by contradiction, DO name the negated conclusion, derive the specific estimate or structure that cannot coexist with it, and identify the contradiction explicitly. Avoid ending with “impossible” unless the incompatible statements are already adjacent and unambiguous.

6.5 Theorem and mechanism

State the formal result before a long proof. If the mechanism is simpler than the technical proof, a short idea paragraph may follow. It must state its temporary assumptions and identify the difficulty that the rigorous section resolves.

6.6 Conclusions from prior results

WHEN combining several established statements, DO say what each contributes. Use a short chain such as: the first result supplies the local structure; the second gives uniform control; therefore compactness applies. Avoid citing several items after a conclusion without mapping them to their roles.


7. VOCABULARY RULES
====================

Select words by mathematical function and evidential strength.

7.1 Author actions

Use “we define,” “we consider,” “we choose,” “we construct,” “we prove,” “we show,” “we claim,” “we estimate,” “we apply,” and “we conclude” for deliberate steps performed in the paper. Use first-person singular only for an explicitly personal correction, attribution, or limitation, and only when authorship makes that voice appropriate. Otherwise prefer first-person plural.

7.2 Consequence vocabulary

Use “it follows that” when the conclusion is a direct consequence of stated premises. Use “thus” or “therefore” when closing a local chain. Use “hence” for a compact consequence. Use “in particular” for a specialization. Use “moreover” for an additional conclusion at the same logical level. Do not rotate these words merely for variety; each must name a real relation.

7.3 Contrast and cases

Use “however” for a genuine obstruction or exception. Use “on the other hand” to place a competing case, estimate, or mechanism beside the first. Use “otherwise” for the remaining logical alternative. Avoid contrast markers when the second sentence merely continues the first.

7.4 Proof support

Use “indeed” to supply the verification of the immediately preceding claim. Use “recall that” only for a specific fact already established or cited. Use “observe that” or “note that” to draw attention to a local property whose relevance is immediate. Use “clearly” and “it is easy to see” only when the omitted step is short for the intended audience and contains no hidden assumption.

7.5 Claim strength

Use “prove” or “establish” for a result supported by a complete argument. Use “show” for either a formal proof or a direct derivation, but ensure the supporting material is present. Use “claim” for a local statement whose proof follows or whose status is explicitly identified. Use “propose” for a new method, definition, or conjectural program without claiming its theorem. Use “suggest” or “is consistent with” for heuristic or numerical evidence.

7.6 Restrained evaluation

Words such as simple, natural, important, remarkable, or useful may appear only when the next sentence explains the mathematical reason. Avoid strings of evaluative adjectives. Prefer the exact property: the argument uses only prior estimates; the normalization removes a scale; the condition cannot be dropped; the conclusion answers the stated question.

7.7 Limitations and corrections

Use direct words: unproved, unjustified, deferred, not needed, not established here, requires an additional assumption, or remains open. Do not soften a proof gap into “technical details are omitted” unless the omitted argument is known and genuinely routine.


8. SENTENCE ARCHITECTURE
========================

The recent native profile uses long sentences, but not long paragraphs. Imitate the logical architecture rather than an average word count.

8.1 Condition -> conclusion

WHEN a conclusion depends on one coherent group of assumptions, keep the condition and conclusion in the same sentence. Put the principal mathematical verb in the main clause. If the hypothesis list becomes difficult to parse, state standing assumptions first and reserve the sentence for the new condition.

8.2 Prior result -> present implication

Use: named result and exact content -> condition showing it applies -> present consequence. Keep the citation next to the supplied fact. Do not put the contribution in a subordinate clause after a long literature preface.

8.3 Claim -> verification

Open with the local claim. Follow with “Indeed” and the calculation, comparison, or theorem that verifies it. Close with the exact property obtained. Use this architecture only when the verification is short enough to remain local.

8.4 Alternative -> case analysis

State the exhaustive alternatives first. Give one case, then mark the second with “on the other hand” or “otherwise.” End with the shared conclusion. Avoid repeating common hypotheses inside every branch.

8.5 Obstruction -> repair

State why the intuitive route is not rigorous. Name the object or continuity, regularity, compactness, or compatibility property that fails. Then state the device used to repair it. This architecture is central to the style's candor.

8.6 Estimate chain

Use semicolons when two dependent estimates must remain in one logical unit. Use a new sentence when the second statement begins a new proof task. Avoid semicolons as decoration.

8.7 Information focus

Place the known object or current proof state near the beginning and the new bound, classification, or contradiction in the main clause or at the end. Do not bury the new result inside a parenthesis. Parentheses may carry a short clarification, local convention, or exact scope condition.

8.8 Length control

A long sentence is appropriate when separating it would obscure quantifier order or the dependency between assumptions and conclusion. Split it when it contains two independent claims, two unrelated citations, or a change from proof to interpretation. Preserve short sentences for decisive transitions: the desired contradiction follows; the construction is complete; the remaining case is symmetric.


9. RHETORICAL MOVE ALGORITHMS
=============================

9.1 Opening a short paper

WHEN the paper has one main theorem, DO:

exact prior result or object -> unresolved question -> present theorem -> minimal description of the mechanism.

Omit a broad field survey. State immediately whether the result uses new machinery or combines known ideas in a new way.

9.2 Opening a long technical paper

DO:

governing equation or program -> strongest relevant prior results -> precise obstruction -> consequences of removing it -> list of present results in logical order -> candid scope boundary.

Avoid a generic contribution list detached from the proof architecture.

9.3 Introducing a theorem

DO:

local setup -> quantified assumptions -> conclusion -> optional one-sentence relation to the main goal.

Do not mix proof language into the theorem statement unless a definition is inseparable from the claim.

9.4 Introducing a lemma

Explain why the lemma is needed. If a conceptual proof is available, give its mechanism and label any simplification. If the rigor obstacle is substantial, name it and move the repair to a dedicated proof unit.

9.5 Beginning a proof

Restate neither the entire theorem nor the whole Introduction. Select the first object or normalization and begin. If the proof divides naturally, announce the cases or claims before entering details.

9.6 Handling an apparent gap

DO:

identify the exact unsupported step -> state whether it affects the current theorem -> isolate the extra condition or replacement lemma -> revise only the dependent conclusions.

Avoid global apologies and vague assurances.

9.7 Presenting numerical experiments

DO:

question -> setup supplied by the data -> measured quantity -> observed pattern -> comparison with the proved prediction -> limitations of the tested range.

Do not let experiments prove a theorem. Do not generate missing runs or values.

9.8 Closing a proof or construction

Name the final logical event: all cases give the required object; the bound contradicts the assumption; the parameter choices are compatible; the claimed estimate follows. Stop once the target is reached unless a corollary or limitation genuinely follows.


10. PARAGRAPH ENGINES
=====================

Choose one engine for each paragraph.

ENGINE A - Problem localization
Opening: identify the object or inherited result.
Middle: state the exact missing property.
Closing: formulate the question or next target.

ENGINE B - Definition to use
Opening: introduce the object and notation.
Middle: state domain, constraints, and defining relation.
Closing: name the estimate or theorem for which it is needed.

ENGINE C - Local deduction
Opening: recall the applicable fact.
Middle: apply it and compute or compare.
Closing: state the bound, sign, convergence, or contradiction.

ENGINE D - Claim and verification
Opening: state the claim.
Middle: verify it with one compact argument.
Closing: return to the parent proof.

ENGINE E - Case split
Opening: state the exhaustive alternatives.
Middle: handle the first and contrasting cases in order.
Closing: give the common conclusion.

ENGINE F - Construction step
Opening: state the property the component must have.
Middle: choose the ansatz or parameter and verify constraints.
Closing: state what remains to be constructed.

ENGINE G - Heuristic and repair
Opening: announce that the following explains the idea.
Middle: reason under explicit simplifying assumptions.
Closing: identify the precise obstacle and point to its repair.

ENGINE H - Prior work as dependency
Opening: state the needed prior fact.
Middle: explain why its assumptions apply here.
Closing: record the consequence for the present argument.

ENGINE I - Limitation or correction
Opening: state the affected assertion.
Middle: identify the unsupported step or missing condition.
Closing: say which conclusions remain valid and what must be changed.

ENGINE J - Numerical interpretation
Opening: identify the theoretical quantity being tested.
Middle: report only supplied observations with their setup.
Closing: use calibrated language and state the untested boundary.

Keep the paragraph focused on its chosen engine. If the closing sentence initiates a different engine, move it to the next paragraph.


11. SECTION MODELS
==================

11.1 Introduction

Purpose: locate the exact mathematical problem and state what the paper proves, constructs, or proposes.

Opening behavior: begin with the governing object, equation, or previous theorem. In a continuation paper, state exactly what the earlier work established and which question remains.

Typical move sequence: object or program -> decisive prior results -> obstruction -> present result -> mechanism -> scope boundary.

Paragraph sequence: use a small number of problem-localization and contribution paragraphs. A long technical paper may require a result-by-result map, but each item must correspond to a later proof unit.

Claim strength: use strong verbs only for results proved in the paper. Mark conjectural goals, deferred components, and heuristic motivation explicitly.

Math density: enough notation to state the governing equation or central result; postpone technical symbol systems until needed.

Citation behavior: cite the theorem or construction that creates the current starting point. Avoid a chronological survey.

Transition behavior: move by consequence and obstruction, not by topic slogans.

Closing behavior: end with the present program, the first technical task, or a brief dependency-based section map. A generic “the paper is organized as follows” paragraph is optional and usually unnecessary.

Common mistakes: generic importance claims, unqualified novelty, hidden continuation assumptions, and a contribution list that cannot be matched to formal results.

11.2 Literature Review

Purpose: supply dependencies and exact comparisons. A separate literature-review section is usually unnecessary in this style.

Opening behavior: begin with the mathematical issue around which the sources differ.

Typical move sequence: needed prior theorem -> what it supplies -> missing condition or unresolved claim -> present response.

Paragraph sequence: group work by mathematical function, not date or author.

Claim strength: distinguish what a cited source proves from what is commonly expected, conjectured, or used heuristically.

Math density: include the assumption and conclusion needed for comparison; omit unrelated details.

Citation behavior: place citations immediately after the fact attributed to them. Never invent a reference.

Transition behavior: use contrast only when hypotheses, conclusions, or mechanisms differ.

Closing behavior: state the precise opening left for the current work.

Common mistakes: bibliography catalogues, vague “many works,” unsupported priority claims, and citations that do not supply the asserted fact.

11.3 Theory

Purpose: establish the definitions, estimates, structural results, and implications on which the main conclusion depends.

Opening behavior: state the local theoretical task and any standing assumptions.

Typical move sequence: setup -> definition -> auxiliary result -> main result -> consequence or next dependency.

Paragraph sequence: use numbered local units and one-task proof paragraphs. Keep definitions near first use.

Claim strength: theorem, lemma, proposition, claim, and remark must correspond to actual status. Do not label an intended statement as a theorem before proof exists.

Math density: high, but every display must have a role in the surrounding argument.

Citation behavior: cite imported estimates at the application point.

Transition behavior: `now` may reset the proof state; `indeed` verifies; `therefore` closes; `on the other hand` separates a genuine alternative.

Closing behavior: record the exact property delivered to the next section.

Common mistakes: unexplained symbols, distant assumptions, formula chains without a target, and local claims whose proofs are never supplied.

11.4 Methods

Purpose: define a constructive, analytical, or computational procedure precisely enough to support the claimed results.

Opening behavior: state what the procedure must produce or preserve.

Typical move sequence: inputs and assumptions -> construction or algorithm -> ordered parameter choices -> well-posedness or stability step -> output property.

Paragraph sequence: one construction or verification step per paragraph. Make the quantifier order of constants and parameters explicit.

Claim strength: distinguish definition of a method from proof of its convergence, stability, robustness, or complexity.

Math density: use displays for the actual update, variational problem, estimator, or parameter condition. Do not replace a missing specification with prose.

Citation behavior: identify the imported routine, theorem, or baseline method and state exactly what is reused.

Transition behavior: use first/then/finally only when they encode dependency.

Closing behavior: state that the construction has the advertised property, or list the unresolved property still requiring proof.

Common mistakes: presenting a proposal as an analyzed method, hiding implementation choices, and claiming robustness without parameter-independent bounds.

11.5 Results

Purpose: present proved consequences and supplied numerical or computational evidence without mixing their statuses.

Opening behavior: state the question, theorem, or predicted quantity against which the result is evaluated.

Typical move sequence: setup -> exact metric or error quantity -> supplied result -> comparison -> bounded interpretation.

Paragraph sequence: separate rigorous corollaries from numerical observations. Separate each experiment when its setup or question changes.

Claim strength: use prove/show for analytic results and observe/illustrate/is consistent with for computations. Do not infer an asymptotic theorem from finite data.

Math density: include enough formulas to define quantities and comparisons. Tables and figures must be explained by function.

Citation behavior: cite benchmarks or reference results only when provided and verified.

Transition behavior: use `in particular` for theorem consequences and `however` for tested limitations.

Closing behavior: state the exact supported conclusion and the range over which it holds.

Common mistakes: invented runs, missing sample or mesh information, causal language for descriptive evidence, and unproved claims of optimality.

11.6 Discussion / Conclusion

Purpose: record what the paper has established, what remains conditional, and which precise obstruction or extension remains.

Opening behavior: begin with the main proved or constructed object, not a general restatement of motivation.

Typical move sequence: verified result -> scope -> limitation or open task -> optional immediate consequence.

Paragraph sequence: keep it short. Omit the section entirely when the final proof or corollary already provides an honest ending.

Claim strength: preserve every qualification from the formal results. Do not broaden from a tested case to a universal claim.

Math density: low to moderate; repeat a formula only if it identifies the unresolved boundary.

Citation behavior: minimal, limited to a concrete next comparison or inherited open question.

Transition behavior: use consequence and limitation markers directly.

Closing behavior: end on a precise mathematical boundary, not a slogan about future impact.

Common mistakes: ceremonial summaries, new unsupported claims, concealed limitations, and a list of vague future directions.

11.7 Proof / Appendix

Purpose: discharge obligations deferred from the main line without changing their statements.

Opening behavior: identify the exact theorem, lemma, estimate, or technical obstacle being resolved.

Typical move sequence: restate local assumptions -> choose objects -> derive estimates -> handle cases -> close the cited obligation.

Paragraph sequence: preserve numbered local units so the main text can point to the required step.

Claim strength: a deferred proof remains unproved in the main text until the appendix supplies it. Label sketches and omitted routine details accurately.

Math density: high where necessary; include prose at changes of strategy and after decisive estimates.

Citation behavior: cite imported technical results at their exact use.

Transition behavior: make proof state explicit with recall, assume, now, indeed, therefore, and contradiction markers.

Closing behavior: say which earlier assertion is now justified.

Common mistakes: changing the theorem while proving it, adding hidden hypotheses, and claiming a technical lemma without resolving its exceptional cases.


12. FLOW AND TRANSITION RULES
=============================

Transitions should report logical movement.

Use `Now` when the proof state changes: a new case begins, the previous estimate is applied, a normalization is introduced, or the argument returns to the main target. Do not begin every paragraph with it.

Use `Indeed` when the following sentence verifies the immediately preceding assertion. The verification must be local. If it takes several pages, introduce a lemma instead.

Use `Thus`, `therefore`, or `hence` when closing a deduction. Select one according to sentence rhythm; do not stack them.

Use `It follows from ... that ...` when the source of the implication matters. Name the estimate, theorem, or construction.

Use `On the other hand` for the second side of an actual dichotomy, competing bound, or opposing mechanism. Use `However` for a limitation or obstruction. Use `Moreover` for another conclusion supported at the same stage.

Use `Recall` to reactivate a specific established fact. Use `Consider`, `Suppose`, and `Assume` to open new local states. Keep temporary assumptions distinct from standing hypotheses and from assumptions that still require proof.

Allow object continuity to replace a connective. If the next paragraph begins with the quantity just obtained, an additional transition may be redundant.

At the section level, close by naming the result delivered forward. Open the next section by stating what will be done with it. This produces flow through mathematical dependencies rather than through a repetitive roadmap.


13. LATEX CODING STYLE
======================

The three native sources support a compact article structure with AMS mathematics, numbered local units, dense inline notation, and few visual elements. Apply that evidence with modern compilation hygiene.

13.1 Document architecture

Use a standard `article` class unless the venue requires another class. Load only packages needed by the document. For a standalone draft, AMS mathematics and theorem support are appropriate. Avoid inheriting a large template preamble whose commands are unused.

Use sections according to major proof dependencies. Avoid subsections when numbered local paragraphs already provide a clear map. Add a subsection only when a section contains multiple independent mathematical tasks.

13.2 Result blocks

Use consistent theorem, lemma, proposition, claim, definition, example, and remark forms. The source often uses manual bold numbered blocks, but a reliable `amsthm` implementation may reproduce the same compact hierarchy more safely. Do not change the mathematical status when choosing an environment.

Number local units when later arguments refer to them. A short self-contained note may use one proposition and a run-in proof without building an elaborate environment system.

13.3 Displays

Use `equation`, `align`, or their unnumbered variants rather than raw `$$...$$` in new work. Align multi-line derivations at the operative equality or inequality. Number only formulas that are referenced or structurally important. Introduce and interpret each display in prose.

13.4 Labels and references

Although the archival native sources use manual numerical references, use semantic `\label` and `\ref` or `\eqref` in a modern long document when they prevent errors. Choose stable prefixes such as `sec:`, `thm:`, `lem:`, and `eq:`. This is a reliability adaptation, not evidence that the author used those prefixes.

13.5 Citations

Keep citations local to the supplied fact. Use the project's bibliography system consistently. Never fabricate an entry, key, page number, theorem number, or attribution. If the source material gives only an unverified citation need, mark it visibly for resolution.

13.6 Macros

Define a macro only for a recurring semantic object or notation whose consistent rendering matters. Keep names local and readable. Avoid single-letter macros that shadow standard commands or make a file impossible to audit. Remove unused macros before final compilation.

13.7 Figures and tables

The native corpus provides no stable figure or table style. Follow the target venue. Captions must state what is shown and how it bears on the claim. Do not infer visual conventions from the converted PDFs.

13.8 Source quality

Compile without undefined references, missing glyphs, overfull boxes that affect readability, or inconsistent numbering. Do not imitate archival misspellings, manual spacing, old display delimiters, or punctuation defects. Preserve the logical style while using dependable contemporary TeX.


14. CLAIM-STRENGTH CALIBRATION
==============================

Maintain a claim ledger while drafting. Assign every substantive statement to one of the following classes before choosing the verb.

A. PROVED IN THIS DOCUMENT
Required support: a complete proof or a direct consequence of a proved result.
Preferred language: we prove, we establish, the theorem shows, it follows that.
Forbidden upgrade: do not omit a hypothesis or expand the conclusion beyond the formal statement.

B. PROPOSED STATEMENT OR METHOD
Required support: a definition, conjecture, program, or algorithm is actually presented.
Preferred language: we propose, we introduce, we formulate, we conjecture.
Boundary: proposal does not imply well-posedness, convergence, efficiency, or correctness unless separately proved.

C. HEURISTIC ARGUMENT
Required support: an informal mechanism, formal calculation, analogy, or simplified model.
Preferred language: heuristically, formally, this suggests, the following explains the idea.
Boundary: state the missing regularity, compactness, continuity, or error control that prevents the argument from being a proof.

D. ASSUMPTION STILL REQUIRING PROOF
Required support: none yet; it is an input or unresolved assertion.
Preferred language: assume, under the provisional assumption, the argument requires, it remains to prove.
Boundary: track every downstream result that depends on it.

E. CITED STATEMENT
Required support: a supplied and verified reference whose hypotheses match the present setting.
Preferred language: by [reference], the cited theorem gives, the result of [reference] implies.
Boundary: do not strengthen the citation or invent bibliographic details.

F. NUMERICAL OBSERVATION
Required support: supplied computations, with setup and measured quantity.
Preferred language: we observe, the data show for the tested cases, the experiment is consistent with.
Boundary: no universal, causal, or asymptotic conclusion unless independently proved.

G. DEFINITION OR CONVENTION
Required support: an explicit declaration.
Preferred language: define, denote, set, throughout this section.
Boundary: do not phrase a convention as an empirical fact or theorem.

H. OPEN, DEFERRED, OR CORRECTED STATEMENT
Required support: an explicit scope note.
Preferred language: remains open, is deferred, is not proved here, the earlier argument does not justify, the following weaker statement suffices.
Boundary: state whether the main result survives.

Before finalizing a paragraph, compare its strongest verb with its strongest available evidence. Reduce the verb whenever the evidence class is weaker. Never raise the evidence class to preserve style.


15. REVIEWER-DEFENSE BEHAVIOR
=============================

The style answers mathematical objections directly and locally.

15.1 Assumption audit

For each theorem, list the domain, regularity, geometric conditions, parameter ranges, discretization hypotheses, and imported results. Put each necessary condition in the statement or immediately before it. If an assumption may be removable, call that a question rather than silently dropping it.

15.2 Gap audit

If a proof step is unsupported, identify the exact implication that fails. Do not write a vague disclaimer. State whether the gap affects the theorem, only a corollary, or merely a stronger program. Replace the claim with the strongest statement actually justified.

15.3 Quantifier audit

Check the order of constants, small parameters, refinement levels, subsequences, and limiting processes. In a construction, record which choice depends on which earlier choice. In an error estimate, state uniformity explicitly. Many apparent stylistic simplifications are invalid because they alter this order.

15.4 Prior-work audit

For every citation-dependent sentence, ask: what exact result is imported, do its hypotheses hold, and is the conclusion used at the correct strength? If the source is unavailable, leave a verification marker. Do not invent authority.

15.5 Numerical audit

Check that every reported value, rate, figure, and comparison occurs in the supplied data. State the tested range. Separate observed performance from proved complexity or convergence. If raw data are missing, do not approximate them from a plot unless the task explicitly authorizes extraction and uncertainty is recorded.

15.6 Correction behavior

When correcting earlier text, use this sequence: quote or identify the affected claim briefly -> isolate the unsupported step -> state the corrected scope -> explain which later results change -> provide the replacement proof or mark it outstanding. Avoid defensive rhetoric.

15.7 Anticipating objections

Address the strongest relevant objection near the claim: necessity of an assumption, exceptional case, dependence of a constant, nonuniqueness, lack of compactness, or mismatch between numerical and theoretical regimes. Do not add speculative objections unrelated to the proof.


16. DO / DO NOT
================

DO begin with a concrete mathematical object.
DO state the exact unresolved question.
DO attach assumptions to the conclusions they govern.
DO identify the function of each estimate.
DO use local claims to divide a long proof.
DO order choices and constants by dependency.
DO mark the start of a new case or proof state.
DO distinguish theorem, proposal, heuristic, assumption, citation, and experiment.
DO state when a stronger goal is not achieved.
DO identify an unjustified step without euphemism.
DO close with the exact conclusion obtained.
DO keep paragraphs locally focused.
DO allow a logically necessary long sentence.
DO use modern, reliable LaTeX for the inferred structure.

DO NOT open with generic praise of a field.
DO NOT invent assumptions, lemmas, references, data, rates, or definitions.
DO NOT turn a numerical pattern into a proof.
DO NOT call an intuitive calculation rigorous.
DO NOT hide a condition in distant prose.
DO NOT use “clearly” to skip a genuine proof obligation.
DO NOT list related work without a mathematical comparison.
DO NOT announce novelty without naming the new object and property.
DO NOT repeat a result in broader language at the end.
DO NOT use one-use notation merely to shorten a line.
DO NOT infer symbol meanings from another paper.
DO NOT imitate PDF conversion artifacts or archival TeX defects.
DO NOT force a conclusion section when the final proof already closes the paper.
DO NOT call the provisional recent model definitively mature.


17. SECTION-SPECIFIC CHECKLIST
==============================

INTRODUCTION
[ ] Does the first paragraph name the object, equation, or theorem?
[ ] Is the obstruction exact rather than generic?
[ ] Is each contribution tied to a formal result or construction?
[ ] Are deferred and unachieved goals marked?
[ ] Are citations attached to the facts they supply?
[ ] Does the final paragraph lead to the first proof task?

LITERATURE REVIEW
[ ] Is a separate section actually needed?
[ ] Are sources grouped by mathematical function?
[ ] Are assumptions and conclusions compared precisely?
[ ] Is every attribution supplied and verified?
[ ] Does the section end at the opening addressed by this paper?

THEORY
[ ] Are standing assumptions visible?
[ ] Is notation introduced before use?
[ ] Does each lemma have a role in the main proof?
[ ] Does prose state what each decisive display accomplishes?
[ ] Are cases exhaustive and clearly separated?
[ ] Is every claim closed or marked outstanding?

METHODS
[ ] Are inputs, outputs, and parameter choices explicit?
[ ] Is the order of choices correct?
[ ] Are well-posedness, stability, convergence, and efficiency kept distinct?
[ ] Are imported routines or theorems identified at use?
[ ] Does the section verify the advertised property?

RESULTS
[ ] Are proved and numerical results separated?
[ ] Are metrics, norms, meshes, samples, and parameter ranges supplied?
[ ] Are all numbers present in the source material?
[ ] Does each table or figure answer a stated question?
[ ] Is the interpretation no stronger than the evidence?

DISCUSSION / CONCLUSION
[ ] Is this section needed?
[ ] Does it preserve all theorem qualifications?
[ ] Does it separate established results from open extensions?
[ ] Does it name one precise boundary rather than broad future work?
[ ] Does it avoid new claims?

PROOF / APPENDIX
[ ] Is the obligation being discharged identified?
[ ] Are temporary assumptions labeled?
[ ] Are compactness, limiting, and exceptional steps justified?
[ ] Does the ending explicitly return to the earlier theorem or lemma?

LATEX SOURCE
[ ] Are packages and macros used?
[ ] Are mathematical environments consistent with claim status?
[ ] Are displays readable and referenced correctly?
[ ] Are citations and cross-references resolved?
[ ] Does the document compile without material warnings or visual defects?


18. SELF-REVISION PROTOCOL
==========================

Pass 1 - Mathematical invariance
Compare the revision with the source claim by claim. Check hypotheses, quantifiers, domains, constants, inequalities, exceptional cases, and conclusions. Undo any stylistic edit that changes mathematical content.

Pass 2 - Evidence labeling
Assign each substantive statement to the claim ledger in Section 14. Search for strong verbs and verify that their evidence class permits them. Mark assumptions, heuristics, numerical observations, cited facts, and deferred claims explicitly.

Pass 3 - Dependency map
For each section, write the output it must deliver to the next section. For each paragraph, identify its single mathematical job. Reorder only when the new order preserves every dependency.

Pass 4 - Proof-state visibility
At every paragraph boundary, ask what has just been established and what begins next. Add a precise transition when the change would otherwise be hidden. Remove connectives that do not express a logical relation.

Pass 5 - Prose-math audit
Check every display for a lead-in and a consequence. Define new symbols. State the function of decisive estimates. Separate heuristic calculations from rigorous arguments.

Pass 6 - Sentence audit
Locate the main verb in each long sentence. Keep a long sentence only when it preserves a coherent condition-conclusion or estimate-consequence unit. Split independent claims and move side conditions next to the statements they govern.

Pass 7 - Reviewer-defense audit
Search for `assume`, `clearly`, `obvious`, `standard`, `well known`, `suggest`, `prove`, `optimal`, `robust`, and `independent`. Verify every use. Identify hidden parameter dependence, missing references, unproved assertions, and unsupported numerical generalizations.

Pass 8 - Style audit
Remove generic openings, promotional adjectives, detached literature lists, ceremonial conclusions, redundant notation, and repeated summaries. Restore direct mathematical verbs and local proof-task paragraphs.

Pass 9 - LaTeX audit
Remove unused packages and macros. Check theorem status, numbering, labels, references, bibliography keys, display alignment, and compilation warnings. Use modern TeX constructs where they improve reliability without changing the inferred logical style.

Pass 10 - Final fidelity check
Read only the theorem statements, assumptions, conclusions, numerical claims, and citations. Confirm that nothing was invented, strengthened, or silently removed. Then read the paragraph openings and endings to confirm that the proof state remains visible from beginning to end.
