AUTHOR STYLE SKILL
==================

Target voice: present-day mathematical writing in the mature Noga Alon style.

Use this file as a decision system, not as a phrase bank. Preserve the stable
identity—problem-first exposition, exact statements, economical proof flow,
and precise open boundaries—while favoring mature practice. Do not copy a
source sentence, a paper-specific symbol system, an old journal format, or a
collaborator's macro vocabulary. Mathematical correctness and the conventions
of the current venue always override surface imitation.


1. STYLE IDENTITY
=================

The indispensable signature is direct mathematical economy. The prose makes
the problem precise, brings the reader to the result quickly, and spends words
on definitions, distinctions, and logical work rather than ceremony.

1. WHEN a paper introduces its central question, DO begin with the central
   object, a canonical theorem, or a concrete mathematical phenomenon. AVOID a
   broad claim that the area is important. WHY: the intellectual stakes become
   visible through the exact problem.

2. WHEN prior work leaves a gap, DO state the missing parameter range, failed
   equality, unavailable construction, or distance between bounds. AVOID
   saying merely that the literature is incomplete. WHY: a mathematical gap
   should already indicate what a theorem would have to accomplish.

3. WHEN announcing a proved contribution, DO use a direct verb—prove, show,
   establish, construct, or determine—and state the conclusion. AVOID inflated
   adjectives or a long contribution preamble. WHY: force comes from theorem
   content, not promotional language.

4. WHEN a result has conditions, exceptions, asymptotic scope, or a tightness
   qualification, DO attach them to the first full statement of the result.
   AVOID postponing decisive restrictions to a later paragraph. WHY: the reader
   should never have to revise the theorem mentally.

5. WHEN using previous literature, DO name the exact result or technique it
   supplies at the point of use. AVOID a detached catalogue of papers. WHY:
   citations are part of the argument's dependency graph.

6. WHEN a proof advances by an immediate deduction, DO mark the logical job
   briefly with words such as indeed, thus, hence, therefore, or it follows.
   AVOID decorating every sentence with a connective. WHY: a marker should
   certify a relation, not merely create rhythm.

7. WHEN a small fact must be isolated inside a proof, DO state a compact claim,
   prove it locally, and reconnect it to the main argument. AVOID promoting
   every observation to a lemma or leaving the reason for the claim implicit.
   WHY: local claims expose the proof's joints without bloating its hierarchy.

8. WHEN introducing notation, DO define it immediately before sustained use.
   AVOID a large inventory of symbols that the reader must hold in reserve.
   WHY: notation is working memory, not decoration.

9. WHEN displaying mathematics, DO make the display a grammatical part of the
   surrounding sentence and punctuate it accordingly. AVOID an unexplained
   formula floating between paragraphs. WHY: prose states the role of the
   formula; the display carries its mathematical load.

10. WHEN closing a theorem, section, or paper, DO land on the obtained bound,
    contradiction, exact consequence, or unresolved boundary. AVOID a generic
    summary of activity. WHY: closure should change the reader's mathematical
    state.

11. WHEN a conclusion contains future work, DO ask a precise question about a
    constant, exponent, parameter regime, equivalence, or construction. AVOID
    broad invitations to explore the topic. WHY: the frontier should be usable.

12. WHEN choosing tone, DO sound confident about proved mathematics and exact
    about uncertainty. AVOID either blanket hedging or blanket certainty. WHY:
    status words, not emotional intensity, calibrate a claim.

13. WHEN deciding paragraph length, DO keep one coherent mathematical task in
    one paragraph even if it requires several short steps. AVOID splitting by a
    fixed word count or combining unrelated obligations. WHY: mature density is
    functional density.

14. WHEN deciding what to omit, DO remove generic motivation, repeated setup,
    and explanations that perform no new job. AVOID removing parameter ranges,
    provenance, or the one sentence that explains why a construction works.
    WHY: compression must preserve mathematical accountability.


2. MATURITY MODEL
=================

Imitate the mature decision system, not the author's development.

FORMATION tendencies include greater variance: a compact announcement at one
extreme, a broad historical survey at the other, longer sentence tails, and
some period-specific TeX. Retain the early problem-first instinct and direct
claim language. Do not retain old announcement formulas, manual source
formatting, or survey-style historical breadth in an ordinary research paper.

TRANSITION tendencies include more explicit signposting, frequent plural
first-person narration, visible proof decomposition, and substantial variation
from coauthors and topics. Retain the clear case splits, local claims, and
objection handling. Reduce serial uses of recall, finally, therefore, and hence
when the mathematical objects already provide continuity.

MATURE tendencies include a short distance from definition or canonical result
to the main theorem; complete parameter calibration; fewer generic hedges;
dense, single-task paragraphs; compact proof modules; exact problem sections;
and consistent theorem infrastructure in sole-authored work.

WHEN evidence from periods conflicts, DO rank choices in this order:

    stable cross-period identity
    > mature same-section practice
    > mature sole-authored practice
    > mature representative coauthored practice
    > transition practice retained later
    > formation-only or venue-specific practice.

Do not impose artificial brevity. Mature sentences still average ordinary
mathematical length, and mature paragraphs can be dense. The refinement is a
narrower long-sentence tail, fewer rhetorical detours, and clearer assignment
of one task to each paragraph.


3. GLOBAL WRITING PRINCIPLES
============================

PRINCIPLE A — Let the mathematics supply the motivation.

WHEN the reader needs a reason to care, DO show a familiar theorem that stops
at a boundary, a conjecture contradicted in a specific regime, two notions that
turn out to be equivalent, or a construction missing an exact property. AVOID
generic statements about wide applicability. WHY: a live mathematical tension
is stronger and shorter than ceremonial motivation.

PRINCIPLE B — State before advertising.

WHEN presenting the contribution, DO give the objects, quantifiers, conclusion,
and strength first. Then add one sentence about consequence or method. AVOID
announcing that a major result is coming before the reader can inspect it. WHY:
the theorem should justify its own emphasis.

PRINCIPLE C — Separate provenance from novelty.

WHEN a paragraph combines known and new material, DO mark each boundary:
attribute the known theorem, state the remaining issue, then state what is
proved here. AVOID a grammatical construction that could make a cited fact
look new. WHY: priority and logical dependency must remain auditable.

PRINCIPLE D — Give every qualification a mathematical address.

WHEN a statement is only asymptotic, probabilistic, conditional, one-sided, or
tight up to a factor, DO place that qualification next to the affected noun,
bound, or quantifier. AVOID a sentence-final hedge whose scope is ambiguous.
WHY: local scope prevents accidental overclaiming.

PRINCIPLE E — Build proofs from named obligations.

WHEN a proof has more than one real bottleneck, DO reveal its architecture:
state the setup, isolate the key claim or lemma, prove it, and say exactly how
it finishes the theorem. AVOID a continuous wall of calculations with no
indication of which step carries the idea. WHY: proof economy depends on seeing
which facts are structural and which are bookkeeping.

PRINCIPLE F — Use examples as tests, not ornaments.

WHEN a definition is abstract, DO give a smallest natural case, a tight example,
or a construction showing why a condition is necessary. AVOID an example that
does not change understanding of the statement. WHY: the preferred examples
locate the theorem's boundary.

PRINCIPLE G — Make comparisons commensurable.

WHEN claiming improvement, DO put the old and new bounds in the same notation
and under the same assumptions. AVOID “better” without naming the dimension,
constant, exponent, or regime. WHY: improvement is a mathematical relation.

PRINCIPLE H — Explain equivalences in both directions.

WHEN replacing one formulation by another, DO state the map and give a brief
argument for each implication unless one direction is genuinely immediate.
AVOID invoking an “equivalent” formulation as a black box. WHY: the equivalence
often carries the conceptual contribution.

PRINCIPLE I — Spend transition words only on transitions.

WHEN the next statement follows, contrasts, specializes, or adds an independent
fact, DO choose the connective that names that relation. AVOID varying words
merely to avoid repetition. WHY: inferential vocabulary is functional.

PRINCIPLE J — Prefer exact modesty to vague caution.

WHEN something is unknown, DO say what is unknown. WHEN a proof supplies only
one side, DO identify that side. AVOID “it seems that more work may be needed.”
WHY: an exact limitation is informative and confident at the same time.

PRINCIPLE K — End at the frontier.

WHEN the main proof is complete, DO use the conclusion to identify what the
argument does not determine and why the remaining gap is natural. AVOID a long
recapitulation of definitions and section order. WHY: the final paragraph
should point to the next mathematical decision.


4. MATHEMATICAL NOTATION
========================

SYMBOL GRAMMAR

WHEN naming a family of sets, graphs, events, or combinatorial objects, DO
prefer a calligraphic capital if that distinction helps separate the family
from its members. WHEN naming an individual graph, set, matrix, or ambient
structure, DO use a plain capital. WHEN naming elements, indices, counts, and
parameters, DO use conventional lower-case symbols. AVOID assigning a fixed
universal meaning to any literal letter; topic conventions take precedence.

WHEN several indexed objects play parallel roles, DO keep the index order
stable and explain each index's range once. AVOID changing between subscripts,
superscripts, and primes without a mathematical reason. Use primes for a
modified or competing object, subscripts for membership in a sequence or
parameterization, and stars only for an optimum, dual, or distinguished object
whose meaning is stated explicitly.

WHEN using hats, bars, or tildes, DO reserve each morphology for one relation in
the paper—approximation, average, closure, lift, or modification. AVOID several
decorations that merely save letters. The reader should infer a relationship
from the decoration after seeing it once.

NOTATION INTRODUCTION

WHEN an object will be used repeatedly, DO write “Let ... denote ...” or define
it in an equally direct sentence, including its domain and relevant parameter
range. AVOID defining notation in a parenthesis several paragraphs before use.
WHY: local definitions minimize memory cost.

WHEN a theorem requires a block of parameters, DO order them from ambient to
local: universe/dimension, size, structural parameter, error/probability
parameter. Then state all admissibility relations. AVOID introducing a derived
parameter before the quantities that determine it.

WHEN notation is used only once, DO write the underlying expression unless a
name materially simplifies the proof. AVOID one-use symbols that force a
reader to look backward. Introduce an auxiliary object only when it reveals a
proof invariant, repeated expression, or structural transformation.

OPERATORS AND SETS

Use the discipline's conventional operators for probability, expectation,
norms, cardinality, minima/maxima, convex hull, and set operations. Pick one
form—such as `\Pr` versus a blackboard probability symbol—and keep it
consistent. Do not infer a literal operator choice from the authorial style;
consistency and conventional meaning are the stable rules.

WHEN a set is defined by a property, DO make the ambient universe and predicate
visible. WHEN a random choice is made, DO name the probability space or random
object before taking probability or expectation. WHEN an asymptotic term is
used, DO say which parameter tends to infinity and what other parameters are
fixed.

EQUATION ARCHITECTURE

WHEN one line expresses the mathematical step, DO use one display rather than
an alignment environment. WHEN equality or inequality chains reveal the proof,
DO align at the operative relation and keep one conceptual move per line.
AVOID multiline displays whose only purpose is to fit the page.

WHEN a display introduces several new objects, DO precede it with a prose map
or split the definition into stages. AVOID making the reader decode more than
two or three genuinely new roles in one formula. Follow a dense display with
the one consequence or interpretation needed next; do not paraphrase every
symbol.

WHEN presenting bounds, DO keep old and new quantities in a comparable form.
WHEN constants are irrelevant, say so and use a named absolute constant. WHEN
constants matter for tightness, retain them throughout. Never switch between
exact and asymptotic notation silently.

COMPRESSION POLICY

Compress repeated algebra, routine symmetric cases, and standard definitions
that can be cited precisely. Do not compress the central transformation, the
reason an assumption is needed, the direction of an equivalence, or the step
that changes the bound. Move long routine verification to an appendix only if
the main text still states the invariant and the resulting conclusion.


5. PROSE–MATH INTERFACE
======================

BEFORE A DISPLAY

WHEN a display states a definition, DO name the object and say what is being
defined. WHEN it states a consequence, DO name the inputs—an earlier theorem,
the preceding estimate, or the current assumption. AVOID “we have” when a more
specific relation such as “the preceding bound gives” is available.

WHEN the reader needs a target, DO state it before the calculation: “It is
enough to prove...” or “We claim that...” Then let the displayed work meet that
target. AVOID calculations whose purpose is disclosed only afterward.

IN A DISPLAY

Treat display math as syntax. Put a comma when the sentence continues and a
period when it ends. Number a display only if it will be referenced or if the
number materially helps navigation. Use simple display environments for simple
statements; use alignment only for real alignment.

AFTER A DISPLAY

WHEN the displayed result closes the local obligation, DO state the exact
landing: it proves the claim, contradicts the assumption, supplies the desired
bound, or permits application of a named result. AVOID “as desired” if the
desired statement is not nearby or obvious.

WHEN a formula has conceptual significance, DO interpret the feature that
matters—monotonicity, sparsity, concentration, integrality, or the dependence on
a parameter. AVOID restating the complete formula in words.

DEFINITIONS AND THEOREMS

Introduce intuition before a definition only when it reduces ambiguity. After
the formal definition, give a special case or equivalence if it will be used.
Before a theorem, provide only the setup needed to read it. After a theorem,
state its relation to the old bound, its tightness, or the proof idea; do not
repeat the statement.


6. MATHEMATICAL NARRATIVE
=========================

DEFAULT RESEARCH NARRATIVE

    central definition or canonical theorem
    -> closest known result or conjecture
    -> exact unresolved range
    -> new theorem with full conditions
    -> one-sentence consequence or tightness statement
    -> proof ingredients
    -> proof
    -> exact remaining question.

Use only the stages the paper needs. A short note may reduce this to
definition -> theorem -> proof -> problem. A broad construction paper may need
known source object -> modification -> invariant -> quantitative verification.

THEOREM HIERARCHY

WHEN a statement is the paper's main deliverable, DO use a theorem. WHEN it is
a reusable ingredient with independent scope, use a lemma. WHEN it is a
secondary result or structural fact, use a proposition. WHEN it follows with
little new work, use a corollary. WHEN it is a local proof obligation, use a
claim. WHEN it is unproved, use conjecture, question, or problem—never theorem-
like prose.

PROOF NARRATIVES

For a construction result, use:

    target parameters -> source object -> modification -> preserved property
    -> quantitative check -> conclusion.

For an upper/lower-bound result, use:

    normalized setup -> key lemma -> bound calculation -> matching example or
    comparison -> statement of tightness level.

For a probabilistic proof, use:

    random object -> bad events or target statistic -> probability/expectation
    estimate -> positive-probability conclusion -> deterministic object.

For an equivalence, use:

    common data -> forward map -> preservation of property -> reverse map ->
    consequence transferred between formulations.

For a contradiction, state the negated target explicitly, derive the forbidden
configuration or numerical violation, and end the paragraph at the
contradiction. Do not hide the contradiction in a trailing dependent clause.

WHEN several lemmas are assembled, DO remind the reader of the role of each in
one short synthesis paragraph. AVOID re-proving or fully restating them. The
synthesis should expose dependency, substitution, and the final parameter
choice.


7. VOCABULARY RULES
===================

RESULT VERBS

WHEN a statement is proved in the paper, DO use “we prove,” “we show,” “we
establish,” “we construct,” or “we determine,” selecting the verb that names
the mathematical act. AVOID “we explore,” “we seek to,” or “we provide
insights into” when a theorem is available.

Use “settle” only when the result resolves the stated question in its claimed
scope. Use “answer” when a posed question is resolved. Use “improve” only with
an explicit comparator. Use “tight” only for an exact match; otherwise write
“tight up to” and name the factor, constant, logarithm, exponent, or order.

PRIOR-WORK VERBS

WHEN reporting a cited theorem, DO write that the named authors proved, showed,
constructed, or asked the relevant statement. AVOID a passive sentence that
blurs provenance. Use “introduced” for a definition or notation, “conjectured”
for an unproved assertion, and “observed” for a limited consequence.

INFERENCE WORDS

- Use “indeed” to supply the promised verification or reason.
- Use “note that” to direct attention to a fact needed immediately.
- Use “observe that” for a short conceptual or structural fact.
- Use “it follows that” when a cited or just-proved statement yields the next
  conclusion without a new idea.
- Use “thus,” “hence,” or “therefore” for a genuine consequence; normally
  choose one, not a stack.
- Use “in particular” for a specialization.
- Use “moreover” for an additional result of comparable status.
- Use “however” for a real contrast, exception, or limit.
- Use “on the other hand” only for a paired alternative.
- Use “clearly” or “it is easy to see” sparingly and only when the omitted
  verification is genuinely short for the intended audience.

HEDGING AND BOOSTING

WHEN mathematics is proved, do not hedge the theorem. WHEN only an extension or
interpretation is uncertain, localize that uncertainty: “it is possible that,”
“we do not know whether,” or “the following remains open.” AVOID “perhaps,”
“apparently,” and “seems” when they do not identify the uncertain proposition.

Avoid ornamental boosters such as “very significant,” “remarkably powerful,”
and “clearly groundbreaking.” Prefer exact evaluators: simple, sharp, strict,
optimal, asymptotic, polynomial, explicit, or elementary—only when each is
mathematically justified.


8. SENTENCE ARCHITECTURE
========================

Use medium-length mathematical sentences as the default. Do not chase a fixed
average. Control the upper tail: if a sentence carries a citation, a condition,
a theorem statement, a comparison, and an implication, separate at least one
of those moves.

STABLE ARCHITECTURES

1. CONDITION -> CONCLUSION: Put the parameter regime or assumption first when
   it controls the whole assertion; put the conclusion in the main clause.

2. KNOWN RESULT -> GAP: State the cited result completely enough to establish
   the boundary, then use a short contrast sentence for what remains.

3. DEFINITION -> IMMEDIATE CONSEQUENCE: Define the object, then give the one
   equivalence or elementary bound that will be used.

4. CLAIM -> REASON -> LANDING: State a local claim, verify it in one or more
   sentences, then say how it advances the theorem.

5. RESULT -> STRENGTH QUALIFIER: State the bound in the main clause; attach
   “with high probability,” “for sufficiently large...,” or “tight up to...”
   exactly where it applies.

6. CONTRAST -> CORRECTED CLAIM: State what might be expected, mark the contrast,
   and put the corrected theorem in the main clause.

7. METHOD -> PROPERTY -> CONSEQUENCE: Name the construction or transformation,
   state the invariant it preserves, then give the bound it yields.

RHYTHM

WHEN a setup sentence must be long because it fixes several parameters, DO
follow it with a short theorem landing or purpose sentence. WHEN several short
proof sentences accumulate, allow one longer synthesis sentence to expose
their relation. AVOID repeated long sentences with nested qualifications.

VOICE AND FOCUS

Use active “we” for actions the paper performs: define, prove, apply, construct,
or claim. Use an impersonal form when agency is irrelevant: “It follows that,”
“It remains to show,” or “It is enough to consider.” Avoid passive voice when
it hides whether a result is new or cited.

Put the new mathematical information in the main clause. Parentheses may carry
a short abbreviation, equivalent formulation, edge case, or citation. Do not
put the central assumption or novelty claim in parentheses. Prefer commas or a
new sentence to a chain of semicolons and dashes.


9. RHETORICAL MOVE ALGORITHMS
=============================

ALGORITHM 1 — INTRODUCE A PROBLEM

WHEN the problem is defined by a new parameter, DO:

    define the parameter
    -> state the immediate general bound or classical special case
    -> identify the conjectured/unknown behavior
    -> state the new result with its regime.

AVOID opening with authorial intentions. WHY: the problem should exist before
the paper's contribution enters.

ALGORITHM 2 — GENERALIZE A CLASSICAL THEOREM

DO:

    state the classical theorem
    -> give its tight example or boundary
    -> define the generalized object/property
    -> state the generalized theorem
    -> compare its strength to the classical case.

AVOID saying “we generalize” until the reader can see what structure is
preserved and what parameter changes.

ALGORITHM 3 — CORRECT A CONJECTURE OR EXPECTATION

DO:

    attribute the conjecture precisely
    -> state the evidence or regime in which it was plausible
    -> identify the counter-regime
    -> state the corrected theorem
    -> quantify how far the correction is from the conjectured value.

AVOID triumphal or dismissive language. A correction is strongest when its
scope is exact.

ALGORITHM 4 — ANNOUNCE A MAIN RESULT

DO:

    one setup sentence
    -> direct proof verb
    -> parameter-complete conclusion
    -> tightness/exception sentence
    -> optional one-sentence method map.

AVOID a multi-item contribution list if a theorem statement can organize the
same information.

ALGORITHM 5 — INTRODUCE A CONSTRUCTION

DO:

    specify the target object and parameters
    -> name the known source object
    -> describe the modification at conceptual resolution
    -> state the property it preserves
    -> defer technical verification to the proof.

AVOID listing construction steps before saying what each is meant to preserve.

ALGORITHM 6 — USE A CITED TOOL

DO:

    state the version needed
    -> verify its hypotheses in the current notation
    -> apply it
    -> translate its conclusion back to the target quantity.

AVOID citing a theorem name as if application were automatic when parameter
matching is nontrivial.

ALGORITHM 7 — DEFEND AGAINST AN OBJECTION

DO:

    state the strongest plausible objection in neutral mathematical terms
    -> concede the exact premise that is true
    -> give the transformation, lemma, or example that resolves it
    -> restate the resulting scope.

AVOID vague assurances or a list of imagined reviewers' concerns.

ALGORITHM 8 — CLOSE WITH AN OPEN PROBLEM

DO:

    recall the proved upper and lower information in one comparable form
    -> identify the remaining gap
    -> ask whether a named value/regime is possible
    -> mention a natural special case only if it sharpens the question.

AVOID “many interesting directions remain.”


10. PARAGRAPH ENGINES
=====================

Choose one engine according to the paragraph's job. A paragraph may be dense,
but it should not change engines halfway through without a visible break.

ENGINE 1 — DEFINITION

WHEN introducing the central object, DO: ambient setting -> formal definition
-> abbreviation/notation -> one immediate example or equivalence. CLOSE with
the property needed next. AVOID a motivational tail unrelated to the definition.

ENGINE 2 — KNOWN RESULT TO GAP

DO: named prior theorem -> exact scope -> limiting parameter/case -> unresolved
question. CLOSE on the gap, not on the citation list.

ENGINE 3 — MAIN RESULT

DO: minimal setup -> direct claim -> full bound -> exception/tightness -> one
consequence. CLOSE by pointing to proof ingredients or the next theorem.

ENGINE 4 — LOCAL CLAIM

DO: “We claim” plus exact statement -> short verification -> consequence for
the enclosing proof. CLOSE with “this proves/supplies...” only when the target
is unmistakable.

ENGINE 5 — PROOF SETUP

DO: fix parameters/objects -> state permissible normalization -> identify the
target inequality or construction. CLOSE with the first actionable step.

ENGINE 6 — CALCULATION

DO: input bound -> displayed chain -> reason for the decisive inequality ->
target bound. CLOSE at the numerical or asymptotic conclusion; do not append a
new conceptual claim.

ENGINE 7 — CONSTRUCTION

DO: source object -> modification -> preservation argument -> resulting
parameters. CLOSE with existence or explicitness, whichever the theorem needs.

ENGINE 8 — EQUIVALENCE

DO: shared data -> forward implication -> reverse implication -> transferred
result. Keep both directions in one paragraph if each is short; otherwise use
two paired paragraphs with mirrored openings.

ENGINE 9 — CASE SPLIT

DO: announce exhaustive regimes -> handle one regime per paragraph -> state the
common conclusion after the last case. AVOID repeating the global setup inside
each case.

ENGINE 10 — TIGHTNESS OR COUNTEREXAMPLE

DO: theorem condition -> boundary construction -> verification -> value it
forces. CLOSE with the exact sense of tightness or necessity.

ENGINE 11 — LITERATURE AT POINT OF USE

DO: mathematical need -> cited result -> relation to current notation ->
application or contrast. AVOID biography, chronology, or unrelated neighboring
work.

ENGINE 12 — OBJECTION AND RESPONSE

DO: precise concern -> why it appears valid -> corrective observation ->
restored conclusion. Keep the tone factual and the response constructive.

ENGINE 13 — OPEN FRONTIER

DO: obtained bound -> best known opposing bound -> unresolved interval -> one
or two exact questions. CLOSE on the sharpest question, not on a generic hope.

PARAGRAPH OPENINGS

Prefer openings that immediately set mathematical state: Let, For every,
Suppose, By the preceding lemma, The following claim, or the name of the object
being continued. Use a transition adverb only when the relation itself is the
new information.

PARAGRAPH CLOSINGS

Prefer a bound, a contradiction, a completed claim, an identified exception,
or a forward obligation. Avoid explanatory afterthoughts after the paragraph
has already landed.


11. SECTION MODELS
==================

INTRODUCTION

Purpose: make the object, prior boundary, new result, and mathematical reason
for the result legible with minimal delay.

Opening behavior: begin with a definition, canonical theorem, or concrete
phenomenon. If the definition is short, put it in the first paragraph. If a
classical theorem is the reference point, state it before discussing the
literature around it.

Typical sequence:

    object/classical result
    -> closest prior bound or conjecture
    -> exact gap
    -> main theorem(s)
    -> tightness or exceptional cases
    -> concise proof idea/organization.

Paragraph sequence: one paragraph establishes the object; one establishes the
known boundary; one gives the principal result; additional paragraphs separate
distinct theorem families or regimes. Do not force a roadmap into a very short
note. In a longer paper, use a roadmap only to clarify proof dependency, not to
repeat section titles mechanically.

Claim strength: direct for proved results; attributed for earlier results;
explicitly conjectural for unresolved extensions. Math density may be high
from the beginning, but every symbol required to read a theorem must be defined.

Citation behavior: cite the nearest theorem, conjecture, construction, or
method at the sentence where it matters. Cluster citations only when they
support the same mathematical fact. Avoid a standalone paragraph that merely
demonstrates coverage.

Closing behavior: end the introduction on the method spine, a concise structure
map, or the first definition needed for the proof. Avoid ending with generic
claims of broad impact.

LITERATURE REVIEW

Default policy: do not create a standalone literature review unless the venue,
scope, or genuine taxonomy requires it. Integrate prior work into the
introduction, preliminaries, or proof at point of use.

When a separate review is necessary, organize by mathematical role—best known
upper bounds, lower bounds, constructions, algorithms, or variants—not merely
by chronology. For each cluster, state: what question it addresses, the best
result relevant here, and what remains different in the present problem.

Claim strength and citations: use named attribution for priority and ordinary
citations for the exact claim. Do not imply that a cited theorem covers the
current setting until its hypotheses have been compared. Close each cluster on
the relation to the present work.

Common mistake to avoid: a long historical opening modeled on a survey. Use
that mode only when writing an actual survey or invited perspective.

THEORY / DEFINITIONS / PRELIMINARIES

Purpose: establish only the concepts and tools that will carry the proofs.

Opening behavior: state the ambient class and fix global notation. Introduce
definitions in dependency order. Put a familiar definition first if it anchors
a generalization; then show the special case that recovers it.

Typical sequence:

    ambient objects
    -> definitions
    -> immediate equivalences/examples
    -> cited tools in the exact required form
    -> first structural lemma.

Paragraph structure: use the definition engine and literature-at-point-of-use
engine. Keep one notation family per conceptual role. Explain why a less
standard condition is present, preferably through a counterexample or the
lemma where it is used.

Claim strength: definitions are exact; observations are local; cited theorems
are attributed; new structural statements receive theorem environments. Math
density can be high, but avoid a page-long symbol catalogue.

Transition behavior: carry forward the defined object by name. Use “note that”
or “observe that” for a property required immediately. Do not repeatedly write
“we now recall.”

Closing behavior: end with the tool or reduction that makes the main proof
possible, not with a summary of definitions.

METHODS / CONSTRUCTIONS / ALGORITHMS

Purpose: expose the mechanism that produces the object or bound. In this style,
“methods” is normally a mathematical construction, probabilistic selection,
reduction, spectral argument, or algorithm—not an empirical protocol.

Opening behavior: state the target guarantee and the available source object.
Name which properties must be preserved and which parameter may deteriorate.

Typical sequence:

    target
    -> source/initial object
    -> modification or random choice
    -> invariant
    -> quantitative analysis
    -> output guarantee.

Paragraph structure: one conceptual operation per paragraph. If an algorithm
has stages, state each stage and its invariant before pseudocode. If a random
construction is used, define the probability space before estimating bad
events. If a reduction is used, prove both correctness directions and report
the parameter transformation.

Claim strength: distinguish existence, constructive existence, deterministic
polynomial time, explicitness, and strong explicitness. Never let one stand for
another. State computational assumptions and asymptotic variables locally.

Citation behavior: cite the source construction or theorem where it enters;
verify compatibility with current parameters. Close on the precise property
obtained, then transition to verification.

RESULTS

Purpose: organize theorem-level output so that scope and relations among results
are immediately comparable.

Opening behavior: state the main result or a compact theorem map. Do not begin
with a prose preview that withholds the actual bounds.

Typical sequence for several results:

    general theorem
    -> special case or corollary
    -> improvement in a restricted regime
    -> matching lower bound/tight example
    -> exceptional or unresolved cases.

Paragraph structure: keep one theorem family or parameter regime per paragraph.
After a theorem, use at most a few sentences to compare it with prior work,
explain tightness, or identify the proof mechanism. Put lengthy proof material
in the proof section rather than between theorem statements.

Claim strength: exact. Say upper bound, lower bound, equality, asymptotic
formula, or probability statement. “Improves” requires a displayed or verbal
comparison under matching assumptions.

Numerical/computational results, if the new paper contains them, are outside
the strongest observed evidence for this style. Report them conservatively:
state whether they are exhaustive computations, simulations, or illustrative
examples; give the range and procedure; and do not promote them to a theorem
without a proof or certified enumeration.

Closing behavior: identify which result is used next, or name the gap that the
proof sections will address.

DISCUSSION / CONCLUSION

Purpose: locate the exact frontier after the results, not retell the paper.

Opening behavior: recover the main result in one sentence only if needed for
the next comparison. Then state what remains undetermined.

Typical sequence:

    achieved bound/equivalence
    -> comparison with the opposing bound or stronger target
    -> precise open problem
    -> optional special case or plausible route.

Paragraph structure: one unresolved family per paragraph. Put upper and lower
bounds in compatible notation. If a small parameter case is known, explain why
it is evidence for the question rather than listing it decoratively.

Claim strength: use “remains open,” “we do not know whether,” or a formal
Problem/Question environment. Use “may” only for an explicitly speculative
method or extension. Do not suggest that numerical proximity implies equality.

Citation behavior: mention later-discovered or priority-relevant work plainly.
Correct earlier attribution without defensiveness. Close on the sharpest
unresolved question.

PROOF / APPENDIX

Purpose: make every dependency and decisive step inspectable while keeping
routine detail proportional to its value.

Opening behavior: restate neither the full theorem nor the introduction. Fix
the objects, normalize without loss where justified, and name the local target.

Typical proof sequence:

    setup
    -> reduction/normalization
    -> key claim or lemma
    -> calculation/construction
    -> synthesis
    -> exact landing.

Paragraph structure: begin with an object, assumption, or dependency. Use local
claims for bottlenecks. Separate cases visibly. End a contradiction at the
contradiction. After proving an auxiliary fact, state exactly where it is used.

Math density: high but narrated. A long display receives a target before it and
a conclusion after it. Routine algebra may be compressed; a non-obvious
inequality needs its reason.

Appendix policy: move long verification, standard variants, or extensive case
checking out of the main line only when the main text retains the statement,
intuition or invariant, and dependency. An appendix is not a place to hide an
assumption or repair an overstatement.

Closing behavior: “This proves...” is appropriate when the referenced target is
clear. Otherwise name the theorem, bound, or case that has been completed.


12. FLOW AND TRANSITION RULES
=============================

SENTENCE-TO-SENTENCE FLOW

Carry an old object into the next sentence before adding new information. A
preferred pair is: establish a property of X; begin the next sentence with X,
that property, or the theorem it activates. Use a connective only when the
logical relation would otherwise be unclear.

PARAGRAPH-TO-PARAGRAPH FLOW

End a paragraph with a result, unresolved obligation, or regime. Begin the next
with that same result as input, the obligation as a claim, or the regime as a
case. This backward-link/forward-task pattern is stronger than a generic bridge
sentence.

SECTION FLOW

At the end of preliminaries, name the tool used by the main proof. At the end
of a construction section, name the property still to be verified. At the end
of an upper-bound section, say whether the next section supplies a lower bound
or a special case. Keep such bridges to one sentence.

CONNECTIVE BUDGET

Use `indeed` for verification, `however` for contrast, `moreover` for an
additional fact, `in particular` for specialization, and one of
`thus/hence/therefore` for consequence. Avoid two consequence markers in the
same short span. Use `recall` only when the recalled fact is not locally
available. Use `finally` only for a genuinely last item in an announced list,
not as a default paragraph opener.


13. LATEX CODING STYLE
======================

PREAMBLE AND MACROS

WHEN a symbol or operator recurs and has one stable meaning, DO define a short
semantic macro. WHEN a macro merely saves a few keystrokes for a one-use
symbol, write the symbol directly. Keep the macro layer small, consistent, and
topic-appropriate. Do not copy any literal personal or collaborator macro list
into a new paper.

Use theorem environments for theorem, lemma, proposition, corollary,
conjecture, definition, problem/question, and claim as required. Share counters
when that improves navigation. Choose environment names and numbering to match
the venue; their rhetorical hierarchy matters more than their internal command
names.

MATH TYPESETTING

Use `equation` for a referenced single display and `align` only for a meaningful
chain or system. Prefer unnumbered modern display environments for unreferenced
math; do not imitate legacy `$$...$$`. Use delimiter sizing consistently and
avoid excessive manual spacing. Put punctuation inside the display according
to the surrounding sentence.

LABELS AND REFERENCES

Use ordinary textual references in the mature spirit: “Theorem \ref{...},”
“Lemma \ref{...},” or “(\ref{...})” according to the venue. Plain `\ref` is the
observed default, while `\eqref`, `\autoref`, or `\cref` may be used if the
project standard requires them. Pick one label-prefix convention for the new
paper, but do not treat any historical prefix as authorial identity.

CITATIONS

Use plain `\cite` unless the bibliography package or venue prescribes another
command. Place the citation immediately after the fact, theorem, conjecture, or
method being attributed. A citation cluster should support one proposition.
Do not use a citation as the grammatical subject when naming the authors would
clarify provenance.

DOCUMENT ARCHITECTURE

Use sections to reflect mathematical modules: introduction/results,
preliminaries, construction or proof ingredients, principal proofs, special
cases, and concluding problems. Use subsections when cases or theorem families
have independent internal logic. Avoid deep nesting created only because the
template permits it.

Split source with `\input` when files correspond to real modules or when team
workflow requires it. Keep definitions and their dependent proofs easy to
navigate. Figures and tables are conditional on content; introduce each in the
prose, give a factual caption, and explain the property it demonstrates.


14. CLAIM-STRENGTH CALIBRATION
==============================

Every assertion must be assigned one status before wording it.

PROVED IN THIS PAPER

WHEN the argument is complete, DO use prove/show/establish/construct/determine
and, when appropriate, a theorem environment. State all conditions and the
exact strength. AVOID suggest, appear, or seem. WHY: hedging a proof obscures
rather than protects it.

PROPOSED BUT UNPROVED

WHEN stating a new conjecture, DO label it Conjecture or say explicitly that it
is conjectured. WHEN asking for a value or possibility, use Problem or Question.
AVOID “we prove” for supporting cases or “we believe” without stating the
mathematical proposition. Separate evidence from the conjecture itself.

HEURISTIC OR INTUITIVE

WHEN an argument only explains why a statement is plausible, DO mark it as
heuristic or intuition and say which step lacks proof. AVOID inferential words
such as therefore if the connection is not deductive. A heuristic may motivate
a construction or conjecture; it may not inherit theorem status.

ASSUMED

WHEN a statement is a standing hypothesis, DO use suppose/assume and state its
scope and duration. WHEN it is without loss of generality, give the symmetry,
normalization, or reduction that justifies the phrase. AVOID silently carrying
an assumption from an example into a theorem.

CITED

WHEN relying on external work, DO name the authors or theorem, state the needed
version, and cite it. Distinguish “proved,” “conjectured,” “asked,” and
“observed.” AVOID wording that lets the reader mistake a cited conclusion for
the paper's contribution. If priority information changes, correct it directly.

NUMERICAL OR COMPUTATIONAL

WHEN a finite computation is exhaustive and independently checkable, DO say
what range was checked, by what procedure, and what conclusion is certified.
WHEN evidence comes from simulations or sampled instances, DO use
“suggests,” “is consistent with,” or “was observed in,” and describe the sample.
AVOID demonstrate/prove/establish for non-exhaustive numerical evidence.

If a numerical calculation is merely arithmetic inside a proof, present it as
a calculation, not an experiment. If code is essential to a theorem, state the
verification contract and make clear which mathematical reduction allows the
finite computation to prove the claim.

NEGATIVE AND OPEN STATUS

WHEN neither direction is known, DO write exactly “we do not know whether...”
or “the problem remains open.” WHEN one direction is known, state it before
asking about the other. AVOID language implying impossibility merely because
the current method fails.

COMPARATIVE STRENGTH

- Exact match: “the bound is tight.”
- Match up to a named loss: “tight up to a constant/logarithmic/factor ...”
- Strict improvement: state old and new quantities under identical assumptions.
- Special-case resolution: say which parameter range is settled; do not imply
  the general conjecture is settled.
- Asymptotic claim: identify the limiting variable and fixed parameters.
- Probabilistic claim: give the probability regime, such as positive
  probability or high probability, and define it if needed.


15. REVIEWER-DEFENSE BEHAVIOR
=============================

Defense should be mathematical and local. Anticipate an objection where the
reader first has enough information to raise it, not in a distant limitations
dump.

WHEN a hypothesis may look unnecessary, DO give a boundary example or state
where the proof uses it. WHEN a construction may look dense, inefficient, or
non-explicit, DO say which property is actually required and, if possible,
show a modification that addresses the concern. WHEN a comparison may be
unfair, align assumptions and notation before claiming improvement.

WHEN a theorem has an exceptional case, DO name it in the statement and handle
its reason nearby. WHEN an equivalence may conceal computational or structural
loss, state the loss in the map. WHEN a cited tool has a non-obvious hypothesis,
verify it explicitly.

Use this objection-response engine:

    possible objection
    -> exact valid premise
    -> observation/lemma/construction answering it
    -> restored scope of the result.

Keep the tone neutral. Avoid “obviously the objection is wrong,” speculative
reviewer psychology, and a string of defensive caveats before the result. The
best defense is a precise theorem, a visible dependency chain, and an example
showing the boundary.


16. DO / DO NOT
===============

DO

- Define the central object early.
- State the best known boundary before the new theorem.
- Use direct proof verbs for completed arguments.
- Put all parameter conditions into the claim's first full statement.
- Attribute conjectures, theorems, and later-discovered work precisely.
- Use local claims to expose proof bottlenecks.
- Give a reason for the decisive inequality or transformation.
- Compare bounds in the same notation.
- Let key objects carry transitions across paragraphs.
- Punctuate displays as parts of sentences.
- End with a precise unresolved boundary.
- Keep macros semantic, local, and consistent.

DO NOT

- Begin an ordinary research paper with a long history of the field.
- Announce importance without showing the mathematical tension.
- Replace a theorem statement with a contribution list.
- Hide an exception or asymptotic regime in later prose.
- Call a near-match “tight” without naming the loss.
- Use “clearly” to skip a nontrivial step.
- Stack indeed/therefore/hence as rhythm markers.
- introduce notation long before use or for one occurrence.
- Split every paragraph merely to make it short.
- Let a citation blur the boundary between cited and new results.
- Treat simulations as proofs.
- Copy venue boilerplate, label prefixes, or collaborator macros as style.
- Close with generic future-work language.


17. SECTION-SPECIFIC CHECKLIST
==============================

INTRODUCTION

[ ] Is the central object, phenomenon, or canonical theorem visible immediately?
[ ] Is the closest known result stated with scope and attribution?
[ ] Is the gap an exact mathematical statement?
[ ] Does the main theorem appear before extended method or literature detail?
[ ] Are exceptions, regimes, and tightness attached to the result?
[ ] Does the final introduction paragraph expose proof structure rather than
    repeat section titles?

THEORY / PRELIMINARIES

[ ] Are definitions ordered by dependency?
[ ] Is every symbol used soon after introduction?
[ ] Are cited tools stated in the exact form needed?
[ ] Is each nonstandard assumption justified by use or example?
[ ] Does the section close on a tool or reduction needed next?

RESULTS / THEOREMS

[ ] Can every result be classified as exact, upper, lower, asymptotic,
    probabilistic, constructive, or computational?
[ ] Are comparisons made under matching assumptions?
[ ] Is each theorem followed by consequence, tightness, or method—not a restatement?
[ ] Are distinct regimes separated cleanly?

PROOFS / METHODS

[ ] Is the local target stated before calculation?
[ ] Are key claims isolated and reconnected to the main proof?
[ ] Does every display have prose preparation and a logical landing?
[ ] Are case splits exhaustive and non-redundant?
[ ] Is “without loss of generality” justified?
[ ] Does the last sentence name what has been proved?

CONCLUSION

[ ] Is restatement limited to what is needed for comparison?
[ ] Are known upper and lower facts written in compatible notation?
[ ] Is each open question precise and status-correct?
[ ] Does the paper end on its sharpest frontier rather than generic significance?

LATEX AND ATTRIBUTION

[ ] Are macros semantic and consistently scoped?
[ ] Are theorem environments used according to claim scale?
[ ] Are only referenced displays numbered?
[ ] Are citations adjacent to the facts they support?
[ ] Can a reader distinguish proved, proposed, assumed, cited, heuristic, and
    numerical statements without guessing?


18. SELF-REVISION PROTOCOL
==========================

PASS 1 — MATHEMATICAL SPINE

Write one line for each stage: object, known boundary, gap, main result, proof
mechanism, remaining question. If a section or paragraph does not serve this
spine or a necessary branch, remove it or justify its function.

PASS 2 — CLAIM STATUS

Tag every substantive sentence internally as PROVED, PROPOSED, HEURISTIC,
ASSUMED, CITED, or NUMERICAL. Revise any sentence whose verb is stronger than
its status. Check that all cited claims have visible provenance and all new
claims have a proof destination.

PASS 3 — PARAMETER AUDIT

For every theorem and comparison, circle quantifiers, domains, exceptional
cases, limiting variables, probability regimes, and tightness losses. Move each
qualification next to the expression it controls. Verify that old and new
bounds use compatible notation and assumptions.

PASS 4 — NOTATION AUDIT

Delete one-use symbols. Standardize decorations and index order. Ensure every
object is defined before use and every operator has a conventional meaning.
For each dense display, ask whether a staged definition or one prose sentence
would reduce decoding cost.

PASS 5 — PROOF-JOINT AUDIT

Mark the key lemma, local claim, reduction, and decisive inequality. If they
are invisible, expose them. If too many statements are promoted to named
results, demote the local ones. After every claim or lemma, state how it enters
the enclosing argument.

PASS 6 — PARAGRAPH FUNCTION

Label each paragraph with one engine from Section 10. Split a paragraph that
performs unrelated jobs. Merge adjacent fragments that jointly prove one small
claim. Move the core mathematical statement to the opening unless setup must
logically precede it. Remove explanatory tails after a clean landing.

PASS 7 — FLOW AND SENTENCE RHYTHM

At every paragraph boundary, check backward link and forward task. Replace a
generic transition with the relevant object, bound, or lemma. Then inspect long
sentences: separate any sentence carrying more than one major inference plus
several qualifications. Preserve occasional short landing sentences.

PASS 8 — WORD CHOICE

Replace vague contribution verbs with prove/show/construct/determine when
justified. Replace generic hedges with an exact open-status statement. Verify
every use of tight, optimal, explicit, simple, or improved. Remove ornamental
boosters and mechanical repetitions of indeed, therefore, hence, recall, and
finally.

PASS 9 — REVIEWER DEFENSE

For each main claim, ask: Are the assumptions necessary? Is the comparator
fair? Is an exception hidden? Is a cited theorem applicable? Is a construction
really explicit in the stated sense? Add the shortest mathematical answer—a
condition, example, verification, or local objection-response paragraph.

PASS 10 — LATEX AND ANTI-OVERFIT

Check display punctuation, numbering, references, citations, theorem counters,
and macro consistency. Remove legacy syntax and venue-incompatible habits.
Finally ask whether any phrase, symbol, label prefix, or section pattern was
copied merely because it appeared in an exemplar. Keep the decision rule;
rewrite the surface form for the new mathematics.

FINAL ACCEPTANCE TEST

The draft is ready only if a mathematically informed reader can answer, without
guessing:

- What is the exact problem?
- What was known, by whom, and in what regime?
- What is proved here, with which conditions and strength?
- What are the one or two ideas that make the proof work?
- Which steps are assumptions, heuristics, citations, or computations rather
  than proofs?
- In what precise sense is the result sharp or not sharp?
- What exact boundary remains open?

If those answers are easy to locate, the text has achieved the intended style:
direct, theorem-centered, logically economical, and exact about both success
and uncertainty.
