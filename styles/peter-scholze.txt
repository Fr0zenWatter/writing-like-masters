AUTHOR STYLE SKILL
==================

Target: Peter Scholze-inspired mathematical exposition
Default language: English
Domain of strongest support: pure mathematics, especially arithmetic geometry,
algebraic geometry, cohomology, and representation theory

This is a decision system for producing new mathematical prose. It does not authorize
copying sentences, notation, or paper-specific terminology from any source. Imitate the
organization of thought: isolate the mathematical need, introduce the object that answers
it, state the exact result, explain its meaning, and expose the proof's reductions.


1. STYLE IDENTITY
-----------------

Retain these features above all others.

1. Begin with the mathematical task. State the question, obstruction, construction, or
   theorem before offering broad disciplinary motivation. [HIGH]

2. Use "we" as the normal reasoning voice, even for sole-authored text. It should mean
   "we now carry out the argument" rather than "we personally feel." [HIGH]

3. Build exposition from formal units: setup, definition, theorem, proof, consequence,
   example, and comparison. Do not separate formal mathematics from its interpretation.
   [HIGH]

4. Introduce machinery because a visible problem requires it. A definition should answer
   a question raised immediately before it, remove an obstruction, or package behavior
   already seen in an example. [HIGH]

5. State important results early and precisely. In a substantial paper, a clean version
   of the principal theorem belongs in the introduction; technical refinements can wait.
   [HIGH]

6. After a central formula or theorem, give its function: what it recovers, why it is
   useful, what it implies, how it compares with a known case, or where it fails. [HIGH]

7. Make the proof's logical economy visible. Say what it suffices to prove, what may be
   assumed, which case is being treated, and what remains. [HIGH]

8. Calibrate verbs by mathematical status. "Prove," "show," "construct," and "define"
   are for completed arguments. "Expect," "believe," "seems," "suggests," and "may"
   mark statements not established in the present text. [HIGH]

9. Use examples as structural evidence. An example should motivate a definition, test a
   bound, reveal an obstruction, or show how a general theorem specializes. [HIGH]

10. Be candid about limitations. Name the hypothesis, failed naive route, missing
    generalization, or scope boundary where it matters, then return to the route that works.
    [HIGH]

11. Prefer conceptual continuity to connective saturation. Carry the important object or
    condition from the end of one sentence or paragraph into the beginning of the next.
    [HIGH]

12. Permit density but preserve one main logical spine per sentence and one main rhetorical
    task per prose paragraph. Mathematical complexity is acceptable; syntactic confusion is
    not. [HIGH]

13. Use "natural" and "canonical" only as mathematical claims about functoriality,
    independence of choices, or uniquely determined structure. Never use them as decoration.
    [HIGH]

14. Keep the TeX source semantically lean. Define commands for recurrent mathematical
    objects, use unnumbered displays by default, and label only items that are referenced.
    [HIGH]

15. Let the last substantive result close the discussion when it already resolves the task.
    Do not append a generic summary merely because academic prose often has one. [MEDIUM]

The desired impression is direct, rigorous, exploratory without being vague, and generous
with mathematical orientation. The text may be technically ambitious, but the reader should
always know why the next object is being introduced.


2. MATURITY MODEL
-----------------

Present-day writing should combine the stable identity above with the mature concept-led
practice described here.

Formation-style writing often spends longer establishing the ambient problem, relies more
heavily on repeated invitations such as "let us first explain," and uses less systematic
source labels. It nevertheless contains valuable traits: toy cases before generality,
precise theorem statements, and short interpretations after dense formal passages.

Transition-style writing reaches the gap and the mechanism more quickly. It places a major
limitation immediately after the result whose reach is limited. Long introductions are
divided by intellectual task: background, results, strategy, examples, or outline.

Mature writing begins from a specific obstruction or unifying question and introduces the
needed object early. It alternates formal statements with explanations, examples, and scope
conditions. It anticipates objections at the point of vulnerability. Its source structure is
more semantic, but its sentence length is not uniformly shorter. Complex comparison theorems
may require long sentences; the mature constraint is a clear logical spine.

WHEN the task is a short note:
DO state purpose, setup, result, proof, and one illuminating consequence with minimal
background.
AVOID monograph-scale history, a giant macro layer, and long taxonomies.
WHY the mature short form is selective rather than merely compressed.

WHEN the task develops a new formalism:
DO expose the obstruction, motivate the category or object, define it, give examples, prove
the structural result that validates it, and then state comparisons or applications.
AVOID presenting a sequence of definitions whose necessity is invisible.
WHY the mature foundational style makes dependency itself the narrative.

WHEN the task has monograph scale:
DO provide a big-picture chapter and explicit conceptual bridges between naive models,
their failures, and improved constructions.
AVOID importing this scale into an ordinary article.
WHY scale-sensitive architecture is evidence of judgment, not a universal surface habit.


3. GLOBAL WRITING PRINCIPLES
----------------------------

Principle A - Need before machinery

WHEN a technical object will be unfamiliar or abstract:
DO first identify the precise operation, comparison, quotient, descent, or finiteness property
that existing objects fail to provide.
AVOID "We now introduce X" without a mathematical need.
WHY the definition becomes memorable when it is the solution to a visible problem.

Principle B - Exact statement before extended proof

WHEN the reader needs to understand the contribution:
DO give a clean theorem or proposition as soon as its notation is available.
AVOID postponing every exact claim until after the full machinery.
WHY a formal target organizes all later definitions and reductions.

Principle C - Interpretation adjacent to formalism

WHEN a theorem, definition, or display is conceptually central:
DO follow it with one of: a plain-language reformulation, a special case, a comparison,
an implication, or an explicit limitation.
AVOID leaving several pages of formal statements without orientation.
WHY the prose-math alternation preserves both rigor and direction.

Principle D - Localize complexity

WHEN a claim requires many hypotheses:
DO collect the hypotheses in one setup or formal statement and keep the surrounding prose
short.
AVOID repeating a full hypothesis list in several consecutive paragraphs.
WHY notation and theorem environments should carry technical load that prose need not repeat.

Principle E - Separate status from aspiration

WHEN a construction points toward a broader program:
DO state the proved local result first, then mark the broader interpretation as evidence,
expectation, or a possible extension.
AVOID allowing programmatic language to blur theorem status.
WHY ambitious motivation remains credible when its logical boundary is explicit.

Principle F - Use failure productively

WHEN the naive definition or proof route fails:
DO explain the exact failure, extract the missing property, and let that property motivate
the replacement.
AVOID reporting failure as an aside with no effect on the exposition.
WHY an obstacle can be the shortest path to the correct definition.

Principle G - One local objective

WHEN starting a paragraph or proof step:
DO decide whether its task is setup, definition, claim, proof, interpretation, example,
qualification, or transition.
AVOID assigning three independent claims to the same paragraph.
WHY technical prose remains navigable when rhetorical roles are visible.

Principle H - End with mathematical closure

WHEN a proof or section has reached its target:
DO state the resulting implication and stop, or identify the one remaining task.
AVOID a ceremonial restatement of everything already proved.
WHY closure should advance the dependency chain rather than fill space.


4. MATHEMATICAL NOTATION
------------------------

4.1 Symbol dictionary policy

There is no universal paper-independent meaning for individual letters. Choose symbols by
mathematical role and maintain a stable local grammar:

- Use ordinary italic capitals for principal spaces, rings, groups, complexes, or functors
  when conventional context distinguishes them.
- Use lowercase letters for elements, maps, indices, and local parameters.
- Use calligraphic letters for sheaves, moduli objects, categories, towers, or geometric
  structures when that distinction reduces ambiguity.
- Use blackboard bold for standard fields, rings, and coefficient systems.
- Use fraktur selectively for ideals, formal schemes, or rings when the distinction is
  semantically useful.
- Use subscripts for base, level, degree, or chosen coefficient data; state the role of a
  nonstandard index when first introduced.
- Reserve superscripts such as star, shriek, flat, plus, or infinity for standard functorial
  or geometric operations. Do not use decoration merely to create another name.
- Use hats, bars, tildes, and primes only after stating what completion, closure, lift,
  cover, or variant they encode.

WHEN several related objects differ by base or level:
DO use one root symbol with disciplined subscripts or superscripts.
AVOID unrelated letters for objects connected by a functorial construction.
WHY visual morphology should display the relationship.

4.2 Introduction order

Introduce data in dependency order:

1. Fix the ambient field, ring, category, graph, or space.
2. Name the principal object.
3. State standing hypotheses.
4. Introduce maps, actions, or auxiliary objects only when the next statement uses them.
5. Define the invariant or functor to be studied.
6. State the result.

Use "Fix" for standing data, "Let" for a local object, "denote by" for a reusable name,
and `:=` for an explicit definitional assignment. Do not introduce five one-use symbols in a
single sentence.

4.3 Equation architecture

- Prefer one conceptual relation per display.
- Use a staged chain only when each equality or implication performs a visible proof step.
- Present general construction before specialization unless the special case is the motivation.
- Use `aligned` inside a display for a small related family; use `align` only when lines need
  separate numbering or extensive alignment.
- Number a display only if it is cited later or is itself a named result.
- Punctuate displays as parts of sentences.
- Put qualifying words such as "canonically," "functorially," or "after inverting p" in prose
  or in the theorem statement, not as unexplained ornaments around the formula.

4.4 Compression policy

WHEN a symbol recurs across a section:
DO define it once and reuse it consistently.
WHEN a symbol occurs once:
DO prefer a descriptive phrase or inline expression unless the formula becomes unreadable.
WHEN a theorem has several compatible outputs:
DO enumerate them under one theorem if they share setup and mechanism.
AVOID compressing distinct logical claims into an opaque single display.

4.5 Dimensions and types

State the domain and codomain of nonstandard maps. State the category or coefficient ring
when it controls meaning. For a familiar conventional object, do not repeat its type at every
use. The reader should be able to check that every displayed composition is well-typed without
being buried under redundant declarations.


5. PROSE-MATH INTERFACE
-----------------------

Use a four-part interface around important mathematics.

Preparation:
Tell the reader what the display or formal statement will accomplish. Introduce only the data
it needs. If the formula solves a previously named obstruction, repeat the key obstruction word.

Formal unit:
Give the definition, equation, proposition, or theorem in its clean mathematical form. Keep
motivation outside the environment unless a short parenthetical condition is indispensable.

Interpretation:
Explain one principal meaning. Suitable moves include:

- identify the familiar theory recovered by specialization;
- paraphrase the condition without duplicating every symbol;
- isolate the decisive case;
- explain why a comparison is surprising;
- say which part of the next proof uses the statement;
- state a limitation or independence-of-choice property.

Continuation:
Let the interpretation create the next task. For example, a theorem establishes existence,
the next paragraph asks about functoriality; an example reveals a failure, the next definition
adds the missing condition.

WHEN intuition is reliable but not itself a proof:
DO mark it with "roughly," "intuitively," or "one may think of," and then return to exact
language.
AVOID extending the heuristic beyond the property it illuminates.
WHY an explicit status marker protects the formal claim.

WHEN a displayed formula is routine:
DO introduce it in one short sentence and continue without a redundant paraphrase.
AVOID explaining every symbol twice.
WHY interpretation is most useful where it changes understanding, not mechanically after all
displays.

WHEN a displayed formula is central:
DO explain why its terms have the stated roles or how it changes the problem.
AVOID "where the notation is obvious" if multiple readings are possible.


6. MATHEMATICAL NARRATIVE
-------------------------

The default narrative is not an uninterrupted chain of lemmas. It is a controlled alternation:

Question or obstruction
-> motivating example or known special case
-> definition of the needed object
-> structural proposition validating the definition
-> principal theorem
-> interpretation or comparison
-> proof reductions
-> consequence, example, or boundary

For a short theorem note, compress this to:

Problem
-> notation
-> theorem
-> proof by reduction and cases
-> sharpness or exactness example
-> conceptual remark

For a construction paper:

Desired operation
-> failure of an existing framework
-> replacement category/object
-> well-definedness or descent theorem
-> functorial properties
-> comparison with known objects
-> application

For a comparison theorem:

Competing theories
-> precise mismatch
-> mediating object
-> list of realization maps
-> theorem of compatibility
-> interpretation of each specialization
-> torsion, finiteness, or boundary consequences

Within proofs, use the following logic openly:

1. State the reduction: "It suffices to..."
2. Normalize data: "We may assume..."
3. Name the key claim if it is not already a lemma.
4. Prove the local or special case.
5. Pass to the general case through the exact functor, descent, limit, or comparison.
6. State the remaining point.
7. Close with the precise result, not with a vague success statement.

Do not call every intermediate observation a lemma. Promote a statement when it is reusable,
has its own hypotheses, or materially shortens later arguments. Keep one-line consequences in
prose.


7. VOCABULARY RULES
-------------------

7.1 Established mathematical actions

Use:

- "we prove" for a theorem whose proof is supplied;
- "we show" for a verified property or shorter argument;
- "we construct" when existence is realized explicitly or functorially;
- "we define" when introducing an object by a complete rule;
- "we deduce" when a result follows from previously established inputs;
- "it follows" when the inference is local and visible;
- "recovers" or "specializes to" for an exact comparison under base change or realization.

Avoid using "demonstrate" as ornamental variation. Repetition of the exact logical verb is
preferable to synonym hunting.

7.2 Conjectural or heuristic language

Use "we expect" for a mathematically informed extension not proved here. Use "we believe"
sparingly for a broader judgment. Use "seems" when evidence is incomplete. Use "suggests" or
"indicates" for a result that points toward a larger correspondence but does not establish it.
Use "may" for possibility, permission, or a non-forced choice, not to hedge a theorem.

Do not write "clearly proves" when the argument merely suggests. Do not use "possibly" where
an exact hypothesis can be stated.

7.3 Logical navigation

Preferred functional phrases include:

- "Note that" for a local consequence or subtle convention;
- "Recall that" for a dependency already known or established;
- "In fact" for a strengthening, correction, or more exact account;
- "In particular" for a selected consequence;
- "More precisely" when replacing an informal statement by an exact one;
- "In other words" for a faithful reformulation;
- "For this reason" when an obstacle motivates the next choice;
- "On the other hand" for a genuine structural contrast;
- "It remains to" for the final unresolved proof task.

Do not insert these at fixed intervals. Each must encode a real relation.

7.4 Evaluative terms

"Natural" should mean compatible with the relevant maps or construction. "Canonical" should
mean independent of choices or uniquely determined in the stated category. "Basic" may describe
a foundational special case. "Key" should identify the mechanism on which later arguments
actually depend. "Useful" should be followed by the use.

Use "easy," "clear," "immediate," and "of course" only when the verification is genuinely
local and can be reconstructed from the adjacent sentence. Never apply them to the main
difficulty, an omitted proof, or a reader's possible confusion.

7.5 Literature language

Attach names and citations to their function: origin of a definition, prior theorem, proof
technique, special case, conjecture, or limitation. Prefer "X proved...", "following X...",
"as in X...", or "compare X..." over a list of loosely related papers. State intellectual
distance precisely: generalizes, refines, recovers, gives another proof of, uses, or is
independent of.


8. SENTENCE ARCHITECTURE
------------------------

The default sentence may be moderately long, but it must have one main clause whose role is
immediately visible. Use subordinate clauses to hold hypotheses, scope, or a reason, not a
second unrelated conclusion.

Stable architectures:

1. Condition -> construction -> exact output.
2. Known result -> missing property -> present goal.
3. General statement -> "more precisely" -> formal formulation.
4. Formal result -> selected consequence -> scope qualification.
5. Obstacle -> reason -> replacement.
6. Definition -> equivalent description -> example.
7. Reduction -> special case -> passage to the general case.
8. Result -> broader indication, explicitly hedged.
9. Long setup sentence -> short landing sentence.
10. Competing viewpoints -> exact reason one is used here.

Information placement:

- Put standing conditions first when they govern the entire sentence.
- Put the new mathematical object in the main clause.
- Put the strongest conclusion near the end only when the preceding setup is essential.
- Move a long technical exception into parentheses or a footnote only if the main sentence
  remains logically true without it.
- Use a semicolon when two clauses are logically parallel or when the second explains the
  first. Do not chain multiple semicolons to simulate a paragraph.

Rhythm:

After a sentence with several hypotheses or a long display, prefer a short interpretive
sentence. After two dense paragraphs, use a compact bridge that states the next task. Do not
force every sentence below a fixed word count; revise instead for visible subject, finite verb,
and one central consequence.

Voice:

Use active "we" for choices, constructions, reductions, and proofs. Use impersonal forms for
intrinsic mathematical consequences: "there is," "it follows," "the map factors," "the action
extends." Passive voice is acceptable when the mathematical object, not the actor, is the topic.
Avoid autobiographical narration except in a short historical remark that explains the method.


9. RHETORICAL MOVE ALGORITHMS
-----------------------------

Algorithm 1 - Foundational introduction

1. State the operation or theory to be built.
2. Name the application or inherited formalism.
3. Identify the class of objects the old formalism cannot handle.
4. Introduce the replacement object.
5. Give the core definition.
6. State the theorem showing the definition behaves correctly.
7. Relate it to classical cases.
8. State scope and set-theoretic or finiteness restrictions.

Algorithm 2 - New unifying object

1. Fix minimal ambient data.
2. State which theories should be unified.
3. Name the key new notion.
4. Define it immediately.
5. Give several examples selected by later comparison theorems.
6. Prove a rigidity or well-definedness property.
7. Define the associated site, functor, or invariant.
8. State comparisons and applications as a structured list.

Algorithm 3 - Gap and reversal

1. State the desired correspondence.
2. Summarize what is known and where it stops.
3. Explain the direction taken by existing constructions.
4. Work in the opposite direction or change the local object.
5. State the resulting functor or theorem.
6. Separate the theorem from the broader correspondence it only suggests.
7. State the exact limitation of the method.

Algorithm 4 - Proposal, failure, replacement

1. Present the naive model fairly.
2. Compute or identify its output.
3. Explain why that output does not solve the intended problem.
4. Extract the missing topology, finiteness, or functoriality.
5. Introduce the improved model.
6. Validate it formally.

Algorithm 5 - Proof by local forcing or cases

1. Fix an arbitrary object.
2. Identify a local invariant with finitely many possibilities.
3. Apply the pigeonhole, compactness, descent, or reduction principle.
4. Split into exhaustive cases.
5. In each case, display the forced structure.
6. State the common conclusion.
7. If optimality is claimed, give a separate counterexample below the threshold.

Algorithm 6 - Reviewer-defense sequence

Claim -> exact hypothesis -> point where the hypothesis is used -> failure without it ->
available weaker variant or explicit nonclaim.

Algorithm 7 - Contribution summary

Use two to four mathematically distinct items. For each, name the object and operation, not a
marketing category. Order foundational construction, structural theorem, comparison, then
application. Avoid repeated claims that differ only in wording.


10. PARAGRAPH ENGINES
---------------------

Choose one engine before drafting a paragraph.

Engine 1 - Task paragraph
Opening: state the mathematical goal.
Middle: identify the minimum setting.
Close: name the object or result that will achieve the goal.

Engine 2 - Obstruction paragraph
Opening: state the desired operation.
Middle: show precisely why the current framework fails.
Close: formulate the missing property, which becomes the next paragraph's topic.

Engine 3 - Definition paragraph
Opening: motivate the object in one sentence.
Middle: give the formal definition, preferably in an environment.
Close: record one immediate consequence or equivalent viewpoint.

Engine 4 - Example ladder
Opening: announce why examples are needed.
Middle: move from familiar to less familiar cases, one role per item.
Close: extract the common feature or the boundary motivating the general definition.

Engine 5 - Theorem setup
Opening: fix data and standing hypotheses.
Middle: define the one invariant or map used in the statement.
Close: introduce the theorem without evaluative buildup.

Engine 6 - Theorem interpretation
Opening: select the theorem part that matters.
Middle: translate it into a comparison, vanishing, finiteness, or classification statement.
Close: say what it permits next or where it stops.

Engine 7 - Proof reduction
Opening: state what suffices.
Middle: justify a normalization or reduction.
Close: isolate the remaining lemma, case, or computation.

Engine 8 - Case paragraph
Opening: name the case and its defining condition.
Middle: carry out only the argument specific to it.
Close: state the forced conclusion in the same vocabulary used in the theorem.

Engine 9 - Comparison paragraph
Opening: name the two theories or constructions.
Middle: give the map and hypotheses under which they agree.
Close: state whether the agreement is equality, equivalence, specialization, refinement, or
only analogy.

Engine 10 - Literature dependency
Opening: state the needed known result.
Middle: cite its precise source and identify the part used.
Close: explain how the present argument changes, extends, or applies it.

Engine 11 - Qualification paragraph
Opening: state the scope boundary plainly.
Middle: explain the mathematical reason.
Close: state the strongest valid claim or expected extension without pretending to prove it.

Engine 12 - Section bridge
Opening: summarize the single output of the preceding section.
Middle: identify the unresolved property.
Close: state the next section's task using the same principal object.

Engine 13 - Sharpness paragraph
Opening: recall the claimed bound or hypothesis.
Middle: construct the counterexample or limiting case.
Close: state exactly why it proves sharpness.

Engine 14 - Closing paragraph
Opening: invoke the final established input.
Middle: make the last inference.
Close: give the theorem's exact conclusion. Stop unless a genuine further question follows.


11. SECTION MODELS
------------------

11.1 Introduction

Purpose: expose the question, core construction, main result, mechanism, and scope.

Opening behavior: begin with the mathematical problem or the operation to be constructed.
For a short paper, give the result in the first paragraph. For a foundational paper, identify
the obstruction and the new object within the first few paragraphs.

Typical sequence: problem -> known boundary -> new perspective/object -> clean definition or
theorem -> interpretation -> applications/comparisons -> limitations -> proof strategy -> outline.

Paragraph sequence: one task per paragraph. Separate gap, construction, theorem, interpretation,
and limitation. Use subsections only when the introduction must independently handle background,
results, strategy, or a big picture.

Claim strength: distinguish the theorem from the program it supports. Use direct verbs for the
former and explicit expectation language for the latter.

Math density: include enough notation to state the result. Do not reproduce the technical proof.
A central definition, comparison map, or theorem belongs here when it organizes the paper.

Citation behavior: cite origins and exact prior boundaries near the relevant claim. Avoid a
standalone catalog.

Transition behavior: let the limitation or proof mechanism motivate the next subsection.

Closing behavior: summarize the proof strategy and section order only if the document is long
enough to need navigation.

Avoid: ceremonial importance claims, delayed contribution, overlong chronology, and a list of
results with no unifying mechanism.

11.2 Literature Review

Purpose: establish mathematical dependencies and intellectual position, not exhaustiveness.

Opening behavior: state the theorem, conjecture, definition, or method needed from prior work.

Typical sequence: known result -> scope -> missing case/property -> present extension or different
route.

Paragraph sequence: group literature by mathematical function or theory. A paragraph may cover
origin, strongest theorem, or competing method, but not all three without a clear spine.

Claim strength: use exact relations such as generalizes, refines, recovers, reproves, or uses.

Math density: include the formal prior statement if the present proof depends on its exact
hypotheses; otherwise describe only the needed content.

Citation behavior: cluster sources only when they perform the same role. Name an author in prose
when attribution matters to the narrative.

Transition behavior: close with the mathematical property still missing.

Closing behavior: lead directly to the present definition or theorem.

Avoid: chronology for its own sake, vague claims that work is "related," and promotional gap
language.

11.3 Theory or Foundational Development

Purpose: create a dependency-ordered formalism.

Opening behavior: fix ambient data and say what operation or property this section must obtain.

Typical sequence: recollection -> definition -> examples -> structural lemma -> theorem ->
interpretation -> comparison.

Paragraph sequence: alternate formal environments with prose that explains role and dependency.

Claim strength: categorical and functorial claims must specify hypotheses and type. Do not call a
map canonical unless independence or universal characterization is present.

Math density: high, but introduce at most a small number of new objects per display or definition.

Citation behavior: cite a known theorem at the moment it is invoked.

Transition behavior: the output of one proposition becomes the hypothesis or object of the next.

Closing behavior: state what construction is now available and where it will be used.

Avoid: a dictionary of definitions without an obstruction, and unexplained notation bundles.

11.4 Methods or Construction Strategy

In this style, "methods" normally means proof strategy, construction, descent, comparison, or
reduction, not empirical procedure.

Purpose: reveal the mechanism without replaying every technical step.

Opening behavior: name the input, target, and principal obstruction.

Typical sequence: local model -> key lemma -> passage to general objects -> independence or descent
-> verification of required properties.

Paragraph sequence: give one reduction or construction stage per paragraph.

Claim strength: distinguish a planned step ("we will reduce") from an established one ("the lemma
gives").

Math density: show the key functor, map, or complex; send routine calculations to lemmas or an
appendix.

Citation behavior: identify imported machinery exactly.

Transition behavior: use "it remains" and "it suffices" to expose the proof graph.

Closing behavior: list the properties verified, then invoke the main theorem.

Avoid: calling a construction obvious, hiding a choice-dependence issue, or confusing heuristic
strategy with proof.

11.5 Results

For theoretical mathematics, results are theorem statements and their consequences.

Purpose: state direction, strength, hypotheses, and relation among results.

Opening behavior: give the principal result before corollaries.

Typical sequence: main theorem -> plain interpretation -> specializations -> corollaries ->
sharpness/limitations -> applications.

Paragraph sequence: do not place multiple unrelated theorems in one prose paragraph. Use a
structured list for several realizations of one construction.

Claim strength: exact. Separate equality, equivalence, fully faithful embedding, bound,
surjectivity, and heuristic analogy.

Math density: sufficient for complete statements; move notation common to several results into a
single preceding setup.

Citation behavior: cite the previous result from which a corollary follows.

Transition behavior: consequences begin from the exact theorem part they use.

Closing behavior: identify the strongest application or the next proof obligation.

Avoid: repeating the abstract, using effect-size language from empirical writing, or listing
corollaries without hierarchy.

11.6 Discussion or Conclusion

Purpose: explain mathematical meaning, scope, and open directions only when they add content.

Opening behavior: begin from the established theorem, not from a generic summary.

Typical sequence: theorem -> conceptual interpretation -> boundary -> expected extension or open
question.

Paragraph sequence: one paragraph may interpret; another may state limitations or future work.

Claim strength: programmatic consequences must be hedged. Do not convert evidence into proof.

Math density: low to moderate; repeat only the central object or comparison.

Citation behavior: cite a broader conjecture or parallel theory if it sharpens the direction.

Transition behavior: explicit only between interpretation and limitation.

Closing behavior: a precise open question or exact final consequence is preferable to a sweeping
claim. Omit the entire section if the last proof already closes the paper.

Avoid: a mandatory recap, generic significance language, and unsupported generalization.

11.7 Proof or Appendix

Purpose: establish correctness while preserving the map of reductions.

Opening behavior: state the reduction or local goal.

Typical sequence: normalize -> split cases -> invoke prior lemma -> compute/construct -> verify
compatibility -> conclude.

Paragraph sequence: one proof step or case per paragraph. Use named claims for reusable substeps.

Claim strength: direct and exact. If a standard step is omitted, identify the theorem that supplies
it or give enough detail to reconstruct it.

Math density: high; displays should align with proof steps and be punctuated.

Citation behavior: cite external inputs at first use.

Transition behavior: "it remains," "we may assume," "by the previous lemma," and "the result now
follows" should correspond to actual edges in the proof.

Closing behavior: restate the exact target established.

Avoid: importing the prose of a contributed appendix into the main authorial voice, hiding a case,
or ending with an unexplained formula.


12. FLOW AND TRANSITION RULES
-----------------------------

Sentence-to-sentence flow:

- Begin from information already active, then add one new object or property.
- Repeat the mathematical noun when a pronoun could be ambiguous.
- Use "this" only with a clear antecedent; prefer "this comparison" or "this obstruction" when
  several antecedents are possible.
- After a formal statement, start the next sentence with the theorem, a numbered part, or its main
  object.

Paragraph-to-paragraph flow:

- End a paragraph by naming an unresolved property.
- Begin the next with the object or construction designed to supply it.
- For a contrast, state both alternatives before selecting one.
- For a change of scale, summarize the local result before moving to global objects.

Section-to-section flow:

- Close with the usable output of the section.
- Open with the next dependency.
- Use a short bridge if the relation is not self-evident; do not write a roadmap paragraph between
  every pair of sections.

Transition strengths:

- Weak/local: also, then, now, in particular.
- Logical consequence: thus, hence, it follows.
- Precision: more precisely, in other words.
- Contrast: however, on the other hand.
- Motivation: for this reason, this leads to, we are therefore led to.
- Proof state: it suffices, we may assume, it remains.

Choose the weakest transition that states the true relation. Avoid "however" when there is no
contrast and "therefore" when the inference has not been supplied.


13. LATEX CODING STYLE
----------------------

13.1 Commands and macros

- Define a macro only for an object, operator, or formatting decision used repeatedly.
- Prefer semantic names such as `\Gal`, `\Spec`, or a domain-specific object name over aliases for
  arbitrary letters.
- Keep the preamble proportional to the document. A short note should not define an alphabet of
  unused calligraphic and fraktur commands.
- Use `\DeclareMathOperator` for genuine operators when available.
- Do not encode paper logic in opaque formatting macros.

13.2 Mathematical typesetting

- Use inline math for short objects and relations.
- Use `\[...\]` for an unnumbered display.
- Use `equation` only when the display is referenced.
- Use `aligned` or `align` for genuine multi-line structure.
- Use delimiter sizing sparingly; prefer ordinary delimiters unless content demands sizing.
- Put commas and periods inside the display according to sentence grammar.
- Break a display where the mathematical structure breaks, not merely to fit a line.

13.3 Theorem environments

Use full semantic environment names in new portable work: `definition`, `lemma`, `proposition`,
`theorem`, `corollary`, `remark`, `example`, `proof`. Number them consistently by section for a
substantial paper. A short note may use global numbering. Do not imitate legacy abbreviations tied
to a class file.

13.4 Labels and references

Use semantic prefixes in substantial work:

- `sec:` for sections;
- `def:` for definitions;
- `lem:` for lemmas;
- `prop:` for propositions;
- `thm:` for theorems;
- `cor:` for corollaries;
- `eq:` for equations;
- `rem:` for remarks;
- `ex:` for examples.

Label only referenced units. Keep labels descriptive and stable under local editing. Use one
reference system consistently (`\ref`/`\eqref` or a configured clever-reference package). Do not
mix systems merely for variety.

13.5 Citations

Use ordinary citation commands appropriate to the selected class. Place a citation next to the
specific attributed claim. Include section, theorem, or page detail when only one part of a long
source is used. Do not reproduce venue-specific punctuation as an authorial rule.

13.6 Figures, tables, and diagrams

The evidence is strongest for commutative or categorical diagrams, not empirical charts. Mention
a diagram before or immediately after it and explain the property it encodes. Captions should state
mathematical content. Use figures only when spatial organization cannot be stated more clearly in
prose or a display.

13.7 Document architecture

Use section depth according to dependency, not visual preference. A normal article should rarely
go beyond subsections. A foundational monograph may use chapters and subsubsections. Put technical
variants, long standard calculations, or separately attributed contributions in appendices and
state their authorship explicitly.


14. CLAIM-STRENGTH CALIBRATION
------------------------------

Proved in the present text:
Use "we prove," "we show," "the theorem gives," "it follows," or an unhedged mathematical
declarative. Supply the proof or an exact dependency.

Constructed in the present text:
Use "we construct" or "we define." State domain, codomain, choices, and whether the construction
is natural or canonical.

Imported result:
Use "by [named theorem/source]" or "recall that." Do not write as if the present paper proved it.

Assumption:
Use "assume," "suppose," or "fix" and specify duration. Distinguish a standing hypothesis from a
temporary proof normalization.

Conjecture:
Use "we conjecture" only for an explicit conjecture. Use "we expect" for a likely extension or
programmatic compatibility. Give evidence if available.

Heuristic:
Use "roughly," "intuitively," or "one may think of." Follow with the exact statement before using
the idea in an argument.

Suggestion from evidence:
Use "suggests" or "indicates." Do not promote it to "establishes."

Numerical or computational evidence:
This corpus does not establish a distinctive numerical-reporting style. State the experiment,
range, observed result, and limitation in neutral exact language. Never present finite computation
as a proof of a general theorem unless a certified exhaustive argument is supplied.

Failure or nonclaim:
Use "does not," "is not known," "we do not pursue," or "the method does not apply" with the exact
reason. This directness is preferable to vague hedging.


15. REVIEWER-DEFENSE BEHAVIOR
-----------------------------

Anticipate objections locally rather than accumulating them at the end.

WHEN a definition depends on size, completeness, finiteness, or topology:
DO state the restriction with the definition and explain whether it is essential or technical.
AVOID hiding it in a later remark.

WHEN a construction uses choices:
DO state whether the output is independent, canonical up to unique isomorphism, functorial, or only
well-defined after fixing the choices.
AVOID calling the result canonical before this check.

WHEN a theorem's hypothesis looks stronger than expected:
DO identify the proof step that uses it, give a weaker variant if proved, or state that removal is
open.
AVOID apologetic language without mathematics.

WHEN a naive route is likely to occur to the reader:
DO analyze it briefly and explain its exact failure before presenting the successful route.
AVOID pretending the alternative does not exist.

WHEN a result resembles prior work:
DO specify whether the new statement changes coefficients, topology, generality, functoriality,
or proof method.
AVOID claiming novelty by adjective alone.

WHEN a proof omits a routine verification:
DO say which identities or prior lemma make it immediate.
AVOID "clear" as a substitute for the missing reason.

WHEN a broad application is only expected:
DO separate it from the theorem in a new sentence or remark and use expectation language.


16. DO / DO NOT
---------------

DO:

- state the problem and result early;
- use "we" for mathematical action;
- fix data in dependency order;
- motivate definitions by an operation or obstruction;
- place central definitions and theorems in visible environments;
- alternate formal statements with interpretation;
- use examples to reveal mechanism or sharpness;
- expose reductions, assumptions, cases, and remaining steps;
- attach citations to exact mathematical functions;
- distinguish proved, imported, assumed, expected, and heuristic claims;
- use precise comparison verbs;
- make limitations local and mathematical;
- use object carry-over for flow;
- prefer unnumbered displays unless a reference is needed;
- keep macros semantic and proportional;
- use semantic labels in new substantial documents;
- stop when the mathematical task is closed.

DO NOT:

- open with generic statements about importance or rapid growth of a field;
- delay the contribution behind an exhaustive literature review;
- introduce machinery before the reader sees the need;
- copy memorable phrases or paper-specific notation;
- use multiple synonyms merely to avoid repeating an exact logical verb;
- call a choice-dependent object canonical;
- call the main step easy, clear, or obvious;
- hedge a proved theorem or state an expectation as fact;
- combine unrelated claims in one long sentence;
- let parentheses contain a second argument;
- explain every trivial display or leave a central one uninterpreted;
- number every equation;
- define macros for one-use symbols;
- imitate journal classes, front matter, or giant collaborator preambles;
- use a monumental chapter architecture for a short note;
- append a generic conclusion after a proof that already supplies closure.


17. SECTION-SPECIFIC CHECKLIST
------------------------------

Introduction:
[ ] Is the mathematical question, construction, or obstruction in the first paragraph?
[ ] Is the main result stated as soon as its notation permits?
[ ] Is the broader program separated from the proved contribution?
[ ] Does every cited work have a precise function?
[ ] Is the mechanism visible before the outline?
[ ] Are limitations adjacent to the relevant claim?

Literature review or background:
[ ] Is prior work grouped by theorem, method, or scope rather than chronology?
[ ] Are exact dependencies and boundaries stated?
[ ] Does the section end at the mathematical opening for the present work?

Theory or foundations:
[ ] Does every definition answer a visible need?
[ ] Are symbols introduced in dependency order?
[ ] Are examples placed before or after definitions for a specific reason?
[ ] Does a structural theorem validate the new object?
[ ] Is each central formal statement interpreted?

Methods or construction:
[ ] Are input, target, and obstruction named?
[ ] Is the local-to-global or special-to-general passage explicit?
[ ] Are choice, descent, and functoriality issues resolved?
[ ] Does the reader know what remains at each major stage?

Results:
[ ] Is the principal theorem first?
[ ] Are hypotheses neither hidden nor needlessly repeated?
[ ] Are equality, equivalence, implication, and analogy distinguished?
[ ] Are corollaries ordered by dependence or importance?
[ ] Is sharpness supported by an example when claimed?

Discussion or conclusion:
[ ] Does the section add interpretation, scope, or an open question?
[ ] Are future directions explicitly non-proved?
[ ] Can the section be omitted without losing mathematical content? If yes, omit it.

Proof or appendix:
[ ] Is the reduction stated before details?
[ ] Are cases exhaustive and separately visible?
[ ] Is each external theorem cited at first use?
[ ] Are displays punctuated and connected to prose?
[ ] Does the last sentence state the exact conclusion?
[ ] Is separately authored material clearly attributed?

TeX source:
[ ] Are only reusable objects macroized?
[ ] Are referenced equations numbered and unreferenced ones unnumbered?
[ ] Are labels semantic and unique?
[ ] Are theorem environments consistent?
[ ] Are no venue artifacts being mistaken for authorial choices?


18. SELF-REVISION PROTOCOL
--------------------------

Pass 1 - Mathematical status

Underline every main verb attached to a claim. Classify the sentence as proved, constructed,
defined, imported, assumed, conjectured, heuristic, or suggested. Replace any verb that overstates
or understates the status. Verify that "canonical" and "natural" have mathematical content.

Pass 2 - Dependency order

For every definition and symbol, ask which previous object it depends on. Move introductions until
the order is acyclic and local. Delete one-use notation that does not shorten the argument.

Pass 3 - Rhetorical map

Label every paragraph: task, setup, definition, theorem, interpretation, example, proof,
qualification, literature, or bridge. Split paragraphs with two independent labels. Merge adjacent
paragraphs only when they perform the same task and share one topic.

Pass 4 - Prose-math interface

Inspect every central display and formal environment. Confirm that the reader knows why it appears
and what its principal consequence is. Remove automatic paraphrases after routine formulas; add
orientation after conceptually decisive ones.

Pass 5 - Proof visibility

Trace the proof as a graph of reductions. Add explicit statements of sufficiency, normalization,
case split, and remaining task where the graph is not visible. Remove connective words that do not
correspond to a logical edge.

Pass 6 - Sentence spine

For each sentence over roughly 35 words, identify the main subject, finite verb, and conclusion.
If there are two independent conclusions, split it. If the length comes only from necessary
hypotheses, keep it and follow with a shorter landing sentence. Resolve ambiguous demonstratives.

Pass 7 - Scope and objections

Search for all standing hypotheses, choices, finiteness restrictions, and omitted cases. Put each
at the point where it matters. State why a naive alternative fails if a knowledgeable reader would
otherwise expect it. Separate open extensions from proved claims.

Pass 8 - Flow

Read only the last sentence of each paragraph and the first of the next. Check that an object,
question, or consequence is carried across. Add a bridge only when the mathematical relation is not
already visible. Remove mechanical "however," "therefore," and "moreover."

Pass 9 - TeX discipline

Delete unused macros. Convert one-use numbered equations to unnumbered displays. Check display
punctuation, label prefixes, reference targets, theorem numbering, and consistent operators. Ensure
the preamble is proportional to the document.

Pass 10 - Anti-overfitting

Replace any phrase that sounds memorized from a particular paper. Check that notation comes from the
new problem. Remove stylistic features justified only by a giant collaborative work, a short
coauthored outlier, or a journal class. Preserve the decision pattern, not the verbal surface.

Final acceptance test:

The reader should be able to answer, at every major point: What problem is active? What object has
just been introduced? What is proved, and what is only expected? Why does the next step follow? If
any answer is unclear, revise before delivery.
